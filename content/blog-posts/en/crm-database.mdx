# CRM database: what it is and how to build one that stays clean

A CRM database is the structured store behind a CRM: one record per person, one per company, one per deal, and the links between them, plus the history of what happened. The links are what separate it from a contact list. Open a company and you see its people, its open deals and the last conversation; open a deal and you see who is involved and what was offered.

You rarely build that database from scratch. You pick a CRM, decide which record types and fields you need, clean your existing data and import it in the right order. This guide covers that design work, shows how four CRMs model their data, and ends with a worked example. Customermates publishes this guide; other vendors' details come from their own documentation, linked at the end.

## What a CRM database holds

Think of it as a handful of tables that point at each other. Most CRMs ship the same core set and differ in what they add around it.

| Record type | What one record is | Typically linked to | Fields worth having |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person (contact) | One human you talk to | Their company, their deals, tasks, messages | First and last name, email, phone, role, how you met |
| Company (organization, account) | One business or institution | Its people and deals | Name, segment, website, billing address |
| Deal (opportunity) | One piece of potential or ongoing business | The people and companies involved, products or services sold | Stage, value, expected start, source |
| Product or service | Something you sell at a price | The deals that include it | Name, price |
| Task or activity | Something someone has to do or did | Any of the above | Assignee, notes |

Two design questions decide whether the database holds up later. Can one person belong to several companies (a consultant who sits on two boards, a freelancer working for three agencies)? And can one deal involve several people? If your CRM forces one company per person or one contact per deal, you will start writing workarounds into notes.

The other half of a CRM database is history. Every change to a record and every linked email or message forms a timeline. That timeline is what lets a colleague take over a client without a handover meeting, and it is the part a spreadsheet cannot give you.

## CRM database, spreadsheet or plain database?

All three can hold customer data. The question is who maintains it and how much of the logic you want to build yourself.

| Choose | When | You give up |
|---|---|---|
| A spreadsheet | One person, a short list, no shared history needed | Links between people and companies, a per-client timeline, safe editing by several people |
| A plain database or no-code tool (Airtable, Notion, Access, PostgreSQL) | Your records are not people and deals, or you need a model no CRM offers | You build the forms, relations, permissions and history yourself |
| A CRM | Several people work with the same clients and you track deals, follow-ups or conversations | Some freedom in the data model; most cloud CRMs charge per user |

If a sheet is where you are now, the [Excel CRM template guide](/blog/excel-crm-template) shows how far one goes and how to lay it out so the later import is easy. For choosing between the kinds of client database tools, see [client database software](/blog/client-database-software).

## How to build a CRM database

These steps apply to any CRM. The order matters more than the tool.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write down the record types you need">Start with people, companies and deals. Add a separate type only when it has its own lifecycle. A product catalogue does; a meeting location does not.</Step>
  <Step title="List the fields you will filter or report on">If nobody will ever filter on a field, it belongs in the notes. For each field pick a type: date, currency, email, phone, link, or a single choice from a fixed list. A "Segment" field as free text ends up with "Retainer", "retainer" and "Ret." within a month.</Step>
  <Step title="Define your deal stages and what moves a deal">Five to seven stages with a written exit rule each, for example "Proposal: a written proposal has been sent". Stages without exit rules turn into opinions.</Step>
  <Step title="Pick one unique key per person">Usually the email address. It is how you find duplicates before import and how the CRM matches incoming email to the right person.</Step>
  <Step title="Clean the source data">One person per row, one kind of value per column, split full names into first and last name, remove duplicates by email, and delete people you no longer have a reason to keep.</Step>
  <Step title="Import companies first, then people, then deals">Each later import can then link to records that already exist, matched by name. Test with 50 rows before the full file.</Step>
  <Step title="Save the views people work from">"My open deals", "Clients without a company", "Not touched in 90 days". A database nobody looks at decays quietly.</Step>
  <Step title="Name an owner for data quality">One person who runs the monthly check below. Without an owner, cleanup is everyone's job and therefore nobody's.</Step>
</Steps>

## When data does not fit contacts, companies or deals

This is the most common design problem, and there are four honest answers.

1. **It describes an existing record.** A contract end date, a client segment, a LinkedIn URL. Add a typed custom field to that record type.
2. **It is something you sell.** Put it in the product or service catalogue and link it to deals, so the deal value follows from what is in it.
3. **It is text you read but never filter.** Meeting notes, background, preferences. Use the notes field.
4. **It has its own lifecycle and relations.** Properties, vehicles, candidates, events. That is a custom object. HubSpot offers them on Enterprise, Salesforce on its platform, and no-code databases such as Airtable are built around them. Customermates does not have custom record types, so data like this belongs elsewhere.

## Worked example: a CRM database for a four-person consultancy

Here is the same design in Customermates, set up the way the product works today. Customermates is an open-source, AI-native CRM built in Germany for small client-facing teams such as consultants, agencies and founders.

**Record types.** Under My Company › Settings › Data model, rename Contacts to Clients and Deals to Projects. Names are chosen from presets, not typed freely. Organizations, Services and Tasks keep their names.

**Custom columns.** Each column has a type: Text, Email, Phone, Link, Date, Date & Time, Date Range, Date & Time Range, Currency or Single Select.

| Record type | Column | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Organizations | Segment (Retainer, Project, Former) | Single Select |
| Organizations | Client since | Date |
| Clients | Role | Text |
| Clients | LinkedIn profile | Link |
| Projects | Engagement period | Date Range |
| Projects | Source (Referral, Inbound, Event) | Single Select |

**Relations.** A client can link to several organizations, and a project to several clients, organizations, services and assigned users. A project's value is the sum of its linked services, each price multiplied by its quantity, so "Strategy workshop" at one price and "Monthly retainer" times six add up on their own.

**Stages.** A new workspace starts with a Stage field (Prospecting, Qualification, Demo, Proposal, Negotiation, Won, Lost) whose win probabilities you set per stage. The weighted pipeline is the plain arithmetic of those percentages, not a prediction.

**Unique key.** Customermates refuses to link one email address to a second contact and shows "This channel is already linked to another contact." Global search (Cmd/Ctrl+K) finds a client by first name, last name, email address or company name.

**Import.** Use Import and export › Add from file with an .xlsx workbook, one record type at a time, in the order organizations, clients, projects. Link columns are matched by record name. CSV files are not accepted, so save them as .xlsx first. The [records documentation](/docs/app-records) has the full import rules.

**Views.** Save "Not touched in 90 days" by filtering Updated at with Older than… 90 days, and "Clients without an organization". Saved views are personal, so each colleague sets the same filters and saves their own view.

**Cost.** Four people on Starter pay [[commercial.price.starter.monthly.seats.4]] per month. On Pro, which adds the [unified inbox](/features/unified-inbox) so each user's connected email, LinkedIn or WhatsApp account shows its conversations next to the client, it is [[commercial.price.pro.monthly.seats.4]] per month. Billing is monthly, and the trial runs [[commercial.trial.days]] days. Self-hosting the open-source core has no licence fee but no inbox and no Mate.

**A routine for data quality (Customermates Cloud).** Mate, the assistant in Customermates Cloud, uses hosted AI credits. A routine runs saved instructions on a schedule or on a record event, as the person who owns it; Pro includes [[commercial.entitlement.pro.includedRoutinesPerUser]] routines per user. A weekly routine could carry this instruction:

> Every Monday, list clients created in the last 7 days that have no linked organization or no email address, and list organizations with no Segment value. Report the lists in the run summary. Do not change any record and do not send any message.

The last sentence matters. Mate can send messages when an instruction asks for it, so a routine meant only to report has to say so. If you prefer your own AI client, Claude, ChatGPT and other MCP clients can query the same records through [[derived.mcp.tools.total]] [MCP tools](/docs/mcp), on Cloud and self-hosted.

## Keeping a CRM database clean

Contact data goes stale as people change jobs and companies merge. A short routine with a named owner beats a yearly cleanup project.

| How often | Check | In Customermates |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | New people without a company, new deals without a stage | Saved views, or the routine above |
| Monthly | Records not updated in 90 days: still relevant? | Filter Updated at, Older than… 90 days |
| Monthly | Free-text values that should be choices | Add a Single Select column, then set it on up to 100 selected records at once with Update |
| Quarterly | People you no longer have a reason to keep | Delete them from the list or the record page |
| Before any big change | A copy of what you have | Export each record type to .xlsx; there is no single whole-account export |

An exported workbook can be edited and read back in: its Record ID column updates the existing records instead of creating duplicates. That makes bulk corrections practical without an API.

## Personal data in a CRM database

In the EU, a CRM database of people is personal data under the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj). Three principles from Article 5 translate directly into database design: keep only data that is "limited to what is necessary" (fewer fields), keep it accurate (the cleanup routine), and limit how long you store it (the quarterly deletion). Article 17 gives people the right to have their data erased, so you need to be able to find and delete one person completely. Article 28 requires a contract with any provider that stores the data for you.

For Customermates Cloud, the [data processing agreement](/dpa) is public, the application database is configured in the AWS Frankfurt region, and the [subprocessors](/subprocessors), US providers included, are listed. Customermates holds no compliance certification. If you need the data on your own server, [self-host the open-source core](/features/self-hosted).

## When Customermates is the wrong database

- **You need your own record types** such as properties, candidates or events. Use a CRM with custom objects or a no-code database.
- **You want documents stored on the record.** Records have no file attachments; notes can embed images and tables.
- **You need field-level permissions.** Permissions apply per record type and action, not per field.
- **You need one-click export of everything.** Export runs one record type at a time as .xlsx.
- **You send newsletters from your customer list.** There is no mass email; use an email marketing tool.

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What is a CRM database?">
It is the store behind a CRM: records for people, companies, deals and tasks, linked to each other, with typed fields and a history of changes and conversations. The links and the history are what make it more than a contact list.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is the difference between a database and a CRM?">
A database stores whatever tables you design and leaves the forms, relations, permissions and history to you. A CRM is a database with that customer logic already built: people linked to companies and deals, pipeline stages, assignees and a timeline per record. You trade some freedom in the data model for not having to build it.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is Excel a CRM database?">
No. Excel can hold a customer list, and for one person with a short list that is enough. It has no links between people and companies, no history per client and no safe way for several people to edit at once. A clean sheet with one kind of value per column still makes a good starting point for an import.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
The usual split is operational (running sales, service and marketing work), analytical (reporting on customer data), collaborative (sharing customer information across teams) and strategic (organizing the business around long-term customer relationships). All four use the same CRM database; they differ in what you do with it.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How do I store data that doesn't fit contacts, companies or deals?">
If it describes an existing record, add a typed custom field. If it is something you sell, use the product or service catalogue. If it is only read, use notes. If it has its own lifecycle and relations, you need custom objects, which HubSpot offers on Enterprise and Salesforce on its platform; Customermates has five fixed record types.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does a CRM database cost?">
A spreadsheet or a self-hosted open-source CRM has no licence fee, but you pay in setup and maintenance time. Cloud CRMs mostly charge per user and month.
