# Customer Acquisition Strategies: How B2B Teams Win New Clients

The customer acquisition strategies that work best for small B2B teams are the ones closest to a real conversation: referrals from happy customers, direct outreach to a short researched list (email, LinkedIn or phone), content that answers your buyers' questions, and events or partners that put you in the same room as them. Pick two channels that fit your buyers, measure what each new customer costs you, and make sure no reply or lead goes without a next step.

This guide explains what customer acquisition (or client acquisition) means, compares the main channels with when each one fits, shows how to calculate customer acquisition cost (CAC) and how it differs from CPA, summarizes the US rules for sales email, and ends with a setup for tracking acquisition in a CRM. Customermates, the CRM that publishes this guide, is where you keep contacts, conversations and deals; it does not send sequences or buy leads for you, and the last section says plainly where another tool is the better choice.

## What is customer acquisition in business?

Customer acquisition is the process of finding people or companies who could buy from you, getting their attention, and turning them into paying customers. Client acquisition means the same thing; the word "client" is more common in services, agencies and consulting.

In B2B it usually runs through four steps:

1. **Find:** decide who your ideal customer is and build a list of companies and people who match.
2. **Reach:** contact them, or make it easy for them to find you.
3. **Qualify:** check whether the problem, budget and timing are real. The guide to [lead qualification with BANT, CHAMP and MEDDIC](/blog/client-qualification) covers the criteria, and a lead that passes becomes a prospect (see [the difference between a prospect and a lead](/blog/prospect-vs-lead)).
4. **Convert:** move the deal through your [sales pipeline stages](/blog/sales-pipeline) to a signed contract.

Acquisition is one part of the larger [customer journey](/blog/customer-journey). Retention (keeping customers) and expansion (selling more to them) come after it, and they are usually cheaper than winning someone new. That is a good reason to make sure your acquisition brings in customers who stay.

## Which customer acquisition strategies work for B2B?

There is no best channel in general, only a best channel for your buyers, your deal size and your time. The table compares the channels most small B2B teams use.

| Channel | Fits when | Effort | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Referrals and introductions | You already have happy customers or a network in the market | Low, but needs asking | Nobody refers unless you ask, at the right moment |
| Personal email outreach | You can name a reason to write to each company | Medium per message | Consent and opt-out rules (see below), deliverability |
| LinkedIn outreach | Buyers are active on LinkedIn and you can comment, connect and message | Medium, daily | Platform limits on invitations and messages |
| Cold calling | Buyers take calls and a customer is worth a conversation | High per contact | Legal rules by country, see the [cold calling guide](/blog/cold-calling) |
| Content and search (inbound) | Buyers search for the problem you solve | High up front, slow to start | Takes months; needs topics people actually search |
| Events and trade fairs | Your market meets in person | High per event | Contacts go cold within days if nobody follows up |
| Partners and resellers | Someone else already serves your buyers | Medium | Shared customers need clear ownership |
| Paid ads | You know your conversion rates and margins | Money instead of time | Costs rise fast without tracking to closed deals |

Most teams do best by picking two channels: one that can produce conversations this month (referrals, outreach or calls) and one that builds over time (content, partners). Running six channels at once with a small team usually means none of them gets the follow-up it needs.

### What are four ways to attract customers?

If you want a short answer: **ask for referrals, reach out directly, publish useful content, and show up where your buyers meet** (events, communities, partners). The first two find customers; the last two let customers find you. Paid ads can speed up either side once you know what a customer is worth.

### Referrals: the cheapest customer acquisition strategy

A referred buyer arrives with trust you did not have to earn. Ask at a moment of success, such as a finished project or a good result, and make it specific: "Do you know one other [type of company] who struggles with [problem]?" Note who referred whom, so you can thank them and see later which customers bring the most new business.

### Direct outreach: email, LinkedIn and phone

Outreach works when every message has a reason that is about the recipient: a hiring post, a new location, a tool they use, something they wrote. Keep the first message short, ask one question, and plan the next touch before you send. When someone replies but goes quiet, the [sales follow-up email templates](/blog/sales-follow-up-email) cover what to write after a call, after no response and to close the loop. For phone outreach, the cold calling guide has an opener, objection answers and the rules in the US, UK, Germany and France.

### Content and search: let buyers find you

Write the answer to the questions your buyers type into Google, AI assistants or ask in communities. One honest, specific page per question beats ten general ones. Inbound takes months, so start it alongside a faster channel, not instead of one.

### Events and partners

At a trade fair, a meetup or a partner webinar you meet many qualified people at once. The value is in what happens after: log every contact with a note and a next step on the same day, and follow up within the week.

## How do you calculate customer acquisition cost (CAC)?

Customer acquisition cost is the total you spend on sales and marketing in a period, divided by the number of new customers you won in that period.

**CAC = sales and marketing cost in the period ÷ new customers won in the period**

What belongs in the cost: ad spend, tools for sales and marketing, event costs, agency or freelancer fees, and the share of salaries spent on acquisition. What does not: the cost of serving existing customers.

**Illustrative example (made-up numbers, not a benchmark):** a small agency spends 3,000 in a quarter on a trade fair and LinkedIn tools, plus 9,000 of salary time on sales and marketing. It wins 8 new clients in that quarter.

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Trade fair and tools | 3,000 |
| Salary time on acquisition | 9,000 |
| Total acquisition cost | 12,000 |
| New clients in the quarter | 8 |
| **CAC** | **12,000 ÷ 8 = 1,500 per client** |

CAC alone says little. Compare it with what a customer brings in. If a client of this agency pays 500 a month with a gross margin of 50 percent, the agency earns 250 a month from that client, and it takes 1,500 ÷ 250 = 6 months to earn back the acquisition cost (the payback period). Calculate CAC per channel too, once you record the source of each customer: a channel with a higher CAC can still be the better one if its customers stay longer.

### What is the difference between CAC and CPA?

CAC (customer acquisition cost) counts only paying customers and includes all acquisition costs. CPA (cost per acquisition, or cost per action) counts whatever action you define, such as a signup, a demo request or a lead, and is usually measured per campaign or ad channel.

| | CAC | CPA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is counted | New paying customers | Any defined action: lead, signup, trial, purchase |
| Which costs | All sales and marketing costs | Usually the spend of one campaign or channel |
| Who uses it | Founders, sales leads, finance | Marketing and ad managers |
| Answers | "What does a new customer cost us?" | "What does this campaign's result cost us?" |

A campaign can have a low CPA (cheap signups) and still produce a high CAC if few of those signups ever pay. That is why the source of each lead needs to stay attached to it until the deal is won or lost.

## Is cold email legal for customer acquisition in the US?

In the US, commercial email to businesses is allowed without prior consent, but it must follow the CAN-SPAM Act. The FTC's compliance guide states that the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. The main rules:

- Honest "From", "To" and "Reply-To" information that identifies you.
- A subject line that matches the content.
- A clear disclosure that the message is an advertisement.
- A valid physical postal address.
- A clear way to opt out, and opt-out requests honored within 10 business days.

Rules elsewhere are stricter. In Germany, advertising by email needs the recipient's prior express consent, also between businesses. In France, B2B email prospecting is allowed when it relates to the recipient's job and the person can object. If you sell into Europe, check each country before you build a list. ## How do you keep new leads from going cold?

Acquisition fails less often at the first message than at the second. A lead replies, a contact from the trade fair says "send me something", a referral introduces you, and then nobody follows up. Four habits fix most of it:

- **Record the source** of every lead (referral, outreach, call, event, content, partner). Without it you cannot calculate CAC per channel.
- **Give every open lead a next step and a date.** A lead without a next step is not in your pipeline, it is in your memory.
- **Keep the conversation next to the lead.** Email, LinkedIn and phone notes on one record mean anyone on the team can pick it up.
- **Review once a week:** which leads have no next step, which replies are unanswered, which deals have not moved.
