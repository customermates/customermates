# What Is CRM? Meaning, Benefits and a Worked Example

CRM stands for customer relationship management. When people say "our CRM", they mean the software: a shared database where every person you deal with, the company they work for, each open deal and the conversations around it are linked, so anyone on the team can see where a client stands and what happens next.

The term also describes a way of working, the decision to organize sales and client work around the relationship rather than around individual inboxes. The German business dictionary Gabler defines CRM as a strategic approach to planning, steering and carrying out all interactive processes with customers. The software is how most companies put that approach into practice. "CRM system", "CRM software" and "CRM tool" all mean the same thing.

Customermates, an open-source CRM, publishes this guide. Other products are named only where they answer a reader's question, and facts about them were checked on 8 October 2026 on each vendor's own pages, linked at the end.

## What a CRM stores

Every CRM is built from a few record types that point at each other. Vendors name them differently, but the model is the same.

| Record | What it holds | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | A person: name, email, phone, role, notes | Laura Kessler, managing director |
| Organization (company, account) | The business the contacts belong to | Kessler Bau GmbH, two contacts linked |
| Deal (opportunity) | Potential revenue with a stage and a value | "Network audit", stage Proposal sent, €12,000 |
| Conversation or activity | Emails, messages, calls and meetings, linked to the records above | Laura's reply from Tuesday asking for a lower scope |
| Task | Work someone has to do, assigned to a person and linked to a record | "Send revised proposal", assigned to Mia |

The links are the point. A spreadsheet can list Laura's name. It cannot show, on one screen, her company, the open deal, the last three emails and who is working on the next step. ## CRM explained with one example

Take a fictional four-person IT consultancy; the names below are invented for illustration. Jonas does sales, Mia and two colleagues deliver projects.

**Without a CRM.** Laura Kessler emails Jonas about a network audit. He replies, has a call, writes notes in a notebook, and sends a proposal for €12,000. Laura asks for a smaller scope. Jonas goes on holiday for two weeks. Mia knows there is "something with Kessler" but not what was promised, the email thread is in Jonas's inbox, and nobody answers Laura. When Jonas is back, she has hired someone else.

**With a CRM.** The inquiry creates a contact and links it to the organization Kessler Bau GmbH. Jonas opens a deal, "Network audit", worth €12,000, and moves it to Proposal sent. His call notes go on the deal. The email thread sits next to Laura's contact record. When Laura asks for a smaller scope, Jonas creates a task, "Send revised proposal", and assigns it to Mia before he leaves. Mia opens the deal, reads the notes and the thread, and answers within a day.

Nothing in the second version needs advanced software. It needs one place where the client's history lives, and a team habit of putting it there. That is what a CRM is for.

## The four types of CRM

Textbooks and analysts split CRM into types. The split describes jobs a CRM does, not separate products you buy. Gabler, for example, names operational, analytical and collaborative CRM. English-language sources often add strategic CRM as a fourth.

| Type | Job | What it looks like in software |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational | Run the daily client work | Contacts, pipeline, email sync, tasks, campaigns, service cases |
| Analytical | Turn the collected data into answers | Dashboards, win and loss by source, segments, revenue reports |
| Collaborative | Give everyone who deals with a client the same history | Shared conversation history, notes, assigned users per deal |
| Strategic | Plan the business around client value | Account plans, retention goals, segment strategy |

Most products sold as CRM software are operational at the core, with reporting on top. For a small team the useful question is not which type to buy but which daily work the system has to carry. ## CRM benefits, and when they don't show up

The benefits of a CRM are real, but each one depends on a condition. If the condition is missing, the benefit is missing too.

| Benefit | What it looks like day to day | Only works if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One history per client | Anyone can answer "where do we stand with Kessler?" | Conversations are logged or connected, not kept in private inboxes |
| Fewer dropped follow-ups | Open deals by stage and assigned tasks are visible to the team | Someone reviews the pipeline every week |
| A pipeline total you can read | Deal values per stage add up without a spreadsheet | Stages have clear entry rules, so "Proposal sent" means the same for everyone |
| Handovers without a briefing call | A colleague opens the deal and has the notes and the thread | Notes are written on the record, not in a notebook |
| Reports that answer questions | Won deals by lead source, average deal size by service | A field such as "Lead source" is filled the same way every time |
| Orderly data protection | Access and erasure requests are answered from one place | Client data is not also kept in copies on desktops |

### The drawbacks

- **Setup takes time.** Somebody has to define stages, fields and who sees what. For a small team that is a day or two, not a project, but it is not zero.
- **Double entry kills adoption.** If logging a call takes longer than the call, people stop. Email and messaging integration, or an assistant that does the logging, matters more than any feature list.
- **Per-seat pricing adds up.** A tool that costs little for one person can cost several hundred a month for a team, especially on the tier that includes email integration.
- **Data decays.** People change jobs and companies change names. A CRM nobody cleans becomes a spreadsheet with a login.
- **Customization can bury the basics.** Twenty mandatory fields on a deal mean nobody creates deals.

## Do you need a CRM yet?

You probably need one when two or more of these are true:

- More than one person talks to the same clients.
- A deal has been lost because a follow-up slipped.
- You cannot say the value of open proposals without counting by hand.
- Client history lives in personal inboxes, WhatsApp chats or LinkedIn messages.
- A new colleague gets up to speed by having emails forwarded to them.

You probably don't need one yet if you work alone with a few dozen active clients and a structured spreadsheet still answers every question you ask it.

## CRM, ERP and spreadsheets

A CRM looks outward at clients and sales. An ERP looks inward at the business: accounting, purchasing, stock and production. A spreadsheet is a list that holds whatever you put in it but links nothing.

| | CRM | ERP | Spreadsheet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main question | Who are our clients and what is open with them? | What did we buy, make, sell and book? | Whatever the columns say |
| Main users | Sales, account managers, founders | Finance, operations, purchasing | Whoever built it |
| Links between records | Contact, company, deal, conversation | Orders, articles, accounts | None, unless you build them |

Many growing companies run both and connect them. Our article on [CRM and ERP](/blog/crm-and-erp) covers how they differ and how data flows between the two.

## Cloud, on-premise or self-hosted

A cloud CRM runs on the vendor's servers and you use it in the browser. The vendor handles updates, backups and keeping it running, and your data sits in their infrastructure. An on-premise or self-hosted CRM runs on a server you control. You decide where the data lives, and you also own updates, backups and security patches. That is also the answer to "what is a CRM server": it is simply the machine the CRM application and its database run on, either the vendor's or yours.

## CRM and data protection

Under the GDPR, a CRM holds personal data, and you need to be able to act on the rights of the people in it. Three rights affect the CRM directly:

| Right | GDPR article | What the CRM must let you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Art. 15 | Find everything stored about one person and hand it over |
| Erasure | Art. 17 | Delete the person and the data linked to them |
| Data portability | Art. 20 | Export their data in a structured, machine-readable format |

Before you choose a vendor, also check where the data is hosted, which subprocessors are involved and whether a data processing agreement is offered. ## What a CRM costs

Free plans exist, with limits. HubSpot's free CRM allows up to two users and 1,000 contacts. Zoho CRM's free edition supports up to three users. Paid CRMs charge per user per month, and three things move the real total more than the headline price:

- whether the price shown assumes you pay a year in advance,
- which tier includes email or messaging integration and AI features,
- onboarding or implementation fees.

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What does CRM stand for?">
Customer relationship management. It refers both to the practice of managing client relationships deliberately and to the software that supports it. In everyday business use, "CRM" almost always means the software.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is CRM, explained with an example?">
In our fictional example, a consultancy receives an inquiry from Laura Kessler. The CRM stores her as a contact, links her to her company, and holds a deal "Network audit, €12,000, Proposal sent" with the call notes and the email thread. When the salesperson goes on holiday, a colleague opens the deal and answers Laura within a day, because the history is not stuck in one person's inbox.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
Operational CRM runs daily sales, marketing and service work. Analytical CRM evaluates the collected data. Collaborative CRM gives every team the same client history. Strategic CRM plans the business around client value. Most CRM products are operational at the core, with some reporting.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is a CRM hard to learn?">
The basics take an afternoon: create a contact, open a deal, move it through stages. What takes longer is the team habit of putting every client conversation in the system. Pick a CRM that connects your email and messaging so logging happens without extra typing, and keep the number of required fields low.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Will AI replace the CRM?">
Not in the sense that the record disappears. An AI assistant needs a structured, current record of clients, deals and conversations to answer anything useful. What AI is changing is who does the data entry and the routine follow-up: assistants can read conversations, update records and draft replies. In Customermates, Mate does this in Customermates Cloud using hosted AI credits, and external AI clients can do it through MCP.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is there a free CRM?">
Yes, with limits. HubSpot's free CRM allows up to two users and 1,000 contacts, and Zoho CRM's free edition supports up to three users. Open-source CRMs can be self-hosted without a licence fee, though you then pay for the server and the upkeep. Check which features the free tier leaves out, especially email integration.
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
