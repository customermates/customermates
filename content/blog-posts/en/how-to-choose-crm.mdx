# How to Choose a CRM: Requirements, Trial Plan and Scoring for Small Teams

To choose a CRM, start with where customer work goes wrong today, not with feature lists. Write down the three to five failures that cost you deals or time, turn each into a requirement you can test, price the plan that actually contains those requirements for a full year, and then test two or three candidates with your own contacts and deals for one working week. Score them with weights, but let any must-have that fails knock a candidate out, however well it scores elsewhere.

Most small teams can do this in two to three weeks. The rest of this guide gives you the tables and scripts for each step. Customermates publishes this guide and makes a CRM; it appears in one clearly labelled section near the end, and the method works the same for any product.

## Step 1: Turn problems into requirements you can test

A requirement such as "good email integration" cannot be tested. "When a client replies, the reply shows up on the contact without anyone copying it" can. The table below shows the conversion for the problems small teams name most often.

| Problem today | Requirement | How to test it in a trial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow-ups depend on someone's memory | Every open deal has a visible next step and an assignee | Create 10 deals, assign them, then filter for deals with no next step |
| Client emails live in personal inboxes | Email (and, if you use them, LinkedIn or WhatsApp) linked to the contact | Connect one real mailbox, reply to a client, check where the reply appears |
| Nobody knows what is in the pipeline | Board view by stage with a sum per stage | Move five deals across stages and read the totals |
| Data is spread over spreadsheets | Import with column mapping into contacts, organizations and deals | Import your real spreadsheet, not the vendor's sample |
| New hires see everything or nothing | Roles with read and edit rights per record type | Create a restricted role and log in with it |
| Fear of being stuck with the vendor | Export of every record type you use, in a format you can open | Export each record type on day one of the trial |

Keep the list short. Each extra must-have removes candidates and adds setup work. A good test: would you refuse to sign if this requirement were missing? If not, it goes on the "later" list.

## Step 2: Separate must-haves from later

Typical must-haves for a team of 2 to 15 people:

- Contacts and organizations with your own fields
- Deals in a pipeline with stages you define
- Conversations from the channels your clients actually use, linked to the right record
- Access rights that match your team
- Import of your existing data and an export you can open without the vendor

Typical "later" items: marketing automation, custom objects, advanced reporting, AI features beyond drafting and summarizing, and integrations with tools you might adopt next year. Some of these will become must-haves. Buy for them when they do.

If you are unsure whether a capability belongs in a CRM at all, our [overview of CRM systems](/blog/crm-systems) explains the usual scope.

## Step 3: Price the full first year

Compare the cost of the plan that contains every must-have, for every user who needs access, over twelve months. Three things change the number more than the headline price.

**The tier your must-haves need.** Email logging, roles, automation or AI often sit one tier above the entry plan.

**The billing term.** Many vendors show an annual-commitment price per month, and some add time-limited offers. HubSpot Sales Hub Starter, for example, lists at $20 per seat per month; when checked, HubSpot showed a time-limited offer of $7 per seat per month on annual billing. Salesforce lists its Starter Suite as billed monthly or annually, and Pro Suite and every higher edition as billed annually.

**One-time fees.** HubSpot's Sales Hub Professional plan lists a required one-time onboarding fee of $1,500.

A worked example for five users on HubSpot Sales Hub Professional, paid monthly at the listed $100 per seat: 5 × $100 × 12 = $6,000, plus $1,500 onboarding, is $7,500 in the first year before tax. The same plan on an annual commitment at $90 per seat comes to $5,400 plus onboarding, but you commit to the full year.

Then add internal time: setup, data cleanup, and training hours multiplied by what those hours are worth to you. Our [guide to CRM costs](/blog/crm-cost) has a full cost model and more vendor prices.

## Step 4: Shortlist two or three candidates by fit

Five candidates is too many to test properly in one week. Use the type of team you are to narrow the field.

| If your situation is… | Look for |
| --- | --- |
| Most deals start from inbound marketing (forms, newsletters, content) | A suite with marketing tools in the same database |
| A sales team works a high volume of similar deals | A pipeline-first sales CRM with activity tracking and calling features |
| Few, long client relationships run through email and messaging (consulting, agencies, advisors) | A CRM that keeps conversations on the contact and the deal, with a simple pipeline |
| You need the data on your own servers or want to read the code | An open-source CRM you can self-host; check which features the self-hosted edition lacks |
| One or two people, under 100 contacts | A [spreadsheet](/blog/excel-crm-template) or a free plan until it stops working |

Industry-specific CRMs (real estate, insurance, recruiting) are worth a look when your process has fixed steps that a general CRM would need many custom fields to model. Our [CRM comparison](/blog/crm-comparison) covers the common general-purpose options side by side.

## Step 5: Run a five-day trial with real data

This is the step most teams skip, and it decides more than all the others. Two or three people who will use the CRM daily should do it, not only the person buying it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Day 1: Import and export">Import 50 real contacts, their organizations and 10 open deals from your current spreadsheet or tool. Then export every record type straight away. If either direction is painful with 50 rows, it will be worse with 5,000.</Step>
  <Step title="Day 2: Set up your pipeline">Replace the default stages with your own, add the three or four fields you actually fill in, and save the filtered list each person needs, such as "my open deals by stage".</Step>
  <Step title="Day 3: Connect conversations">Connect one real mailbox (and LinkedIn or WhatsApp if your clients use them). Send and receive real messages and check that they land on the right contact and deal.</Step>
  <Step title="Day 4: Work a normal day in it">Log every client touch in the CRM for one day. Count the clicks for the three things you do most often. Ask the testers where they gave up and went back to the old tool.</Step>
  <Step title="Day 5: Check access and AI">Log in with a restricted role. Try any AI features on real records: what can they read, what can they change without asking, and what does a day of use cost?</Step>
</Steps>

Write down what happened each day. The notes become your scores in Step 7.

## Step 6: Ask about your data before you sign

Feature questions get answered in the demo. These are the ones that matter after the contract:

- **Where is the data stored, and who processes it?** Ask for the list of subprocessors and the regions they use. A configured hosting region is a setting, not automatically a contractual guarantee, so ask which one it is.
- **Is there a data processing agreement?** If the vendor processes personal data of EU residents on your behalf, Article 28 of the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) requires a contract with that processor.
- **How do I get everything out?** Ask which record types can be exported, in which format, by whom, and whether there are row limits. Do it during the trial rather than trusting the answer.
- **What happens on cancellation?** How long is the data kept, and can you still export it?
- **How does the price change?** Ask about renewal prices, minimum seats, and what happens when you add or remove users mid-term.
- **What does the AI send where?** Which model provider receives your records and messages, and are AI features billed separately from seats?

## Step 7: Score with weights and knock-out minimums

Give each must-have a weight from 1 to 5 and score each candidate from 1 to 5 based on the trial notes. Then set a minimum score for every must-have. A candidate that falls below a minimum is out, whatever its total.

| Criterion | Weight | CRM A score | CRM A weighted | CRM B score | CRM B weighted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Next steps visible on every deal | 5 | 4 | 20 | 5 | 25 |
| Client email linked to records (minimum 3) | 4 | 5 | 20 | 2 | 8 |
| First-year cost for 5 users | 3 | 3 | 9 | 4 | 12 |
| Import and full export | 3 | 4 | 12 | 3 | 9 |
| Testers kept using it | 5 | 3 | 15 | 5 | 25 |
| **Total (max 100)** | | | **76** | | **79** |

CRM B wins on points. It still loses here, because it scored 2 on linked email, below the minimum of 3 that the team set for one of its must-haves. Without knock-out minimums, a pleasant interface can outscore a missing core requirement.

If two candidates both pass every minimum and end within five points of each other, choose the one the testers kept using without being reminded. Adoption decides whether the CRM pays for itself. Our [implementation guide](/blog/crm-implementation) covers the rollout after the decision.

## Red flags during the evaluation

- The must-have you were shown in the demo turns out to need a higher tier than the one you were quoted.
- Export is only possible through support, or only for some record types.
- The vendor offers a guided demo but no trial of the plan you would buy.
- Nobody can tell you where the data is hosted or which subprocessors handle it.
- AI features are billed per use, but nobody can tell you what typical use costs.
- After day 4, the testers have gone back to the spreadsheet.

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What are the most important criteria when choosing a CRM?">
The ones derived from your own problems: usually a visible next step on every deal, client conversations linked to records, a pipeline overview, access rights, and a full export. After that come first-year cost for the tier you need and whether your team keeps using it during the trial. Generic criteria lists are a starting point, not a substitute for testing your own workflow.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
Operational CRM handles daily work (contacts, deals, tasks, communication). Analytical CRM focuses on reporting on that data. Collaborative CRM shares customer context across teams. Strategic CRM is about long-term relationship planning. Some sources list only three types and leave out strategic. Small teams almost always need an operational CRM with basic reporting, and the type label rarely helps choose between products.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the 7 C's of CRM?">
Different publishers use different lists under that name, so there is no single standard version. Most of them describe principles for running a CRM well, such as customer focus, consistency of data and collaboration. They are useful for the rollout, but they are not a selection method; the steps above are.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does an average CRM cost?">
There is no meaningful average, because entry tiers and enterprise editions differ by a factor of more than twenty. On 8 October 2026, HubSpot's Sales Hub Starter was listed at $20 per seat per month and Salesforce's Max edition at $550 per user per month billed annually. Price the tier you need for a full year instead; our [CRM cost guide](/blog/crm-cost) shows how.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Which CRM is best for beginners?">
The one with the fewest concepts you need on day one: contacts, organizations, deals and a pipeline. If you have never used a CRM, start with a free plan or a trial, import a real spreadsheet, and see whether you are still using it after a week. Our guide to [CRM for small businesses](/blog/crm-for-small-business) covers that situation in detail.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Will AI replace the CRM?">
AI changes who does the data entry, not whether you need a place where contacts, deals and conversations are stored. When you evaluate a CRM, check whether an AI assistant or your own AI client can read and update records, which actions need approval, and how AI use is billed.
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
