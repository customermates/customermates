# What Is Key Account Management? A Practical Guide for Small B2B Teams

What is key account management? It is how you run the few customers your business depends on most, on purpose: each one gets a named owner, a written account plan, a map of the people who decide and a fixed review rhythm, while every other customer gets standard service. Choose those accounts with clear criteria instead of gut feeling, and keep plan and history on one record, so the account does not live in one person's head.

This guide separates key account management from general account management, shows how to choose key accounts with a scoring table (a worked example with made-up numbers), lays out a five-step process, and gives you an account plan, a stakeholder map, a review rhythm and a short list of numbers to copy. It ends with a setup in Customermates, the open-source CRM that publishes it. Customermates has no account scoring and no forecasting, so the last section says what you do by hand and when a larger CRM fits better.

## What is key account management?

Key account management is a way of organizing sales and service around a short list of strategically important customers. A key account is a customer you treat differently from the rest because of what it means for your results: a large share of revenue or margin, clear room to grow, or strategic value such as a reference, access to a market or a hard-to-replace partnership. The German Gabler Wirtschaftslexikon, a standard business reference, describes key account management as the preferential treatment of customers that hold a key position for a company's success. The English Wikipedia article on account managers adds that it includes selling but also planning and managing the full relationship with a company's most important customers. Sources and check date are listed at the end.

A key account is therefore not simply your biggest invoice. A customer with high revenue and a thin margin may not qualify, while a small, fast-growing customer with a well-known name might. The next sections show how to decide that with a written rule.

### What is the difference between key account management and account management?

Account management looks after existing customers in general: onboarding, support contact, renewals, the next sale. Key account management applies the same work with more depth to a few accounts. Most small companies need account management first and add KAM for the top handful.

| | Account management | Key account management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who is covered | All or most existing customers | A short list chosen by criteria |
| Goal | Keep customers satisfied, renew, sell more | Protect and grow the accounts whose result moves yours |
| Planning | Follow-up per customer, usually informal | A written account plan per account, reviewed on a schedule |
| Contacts | One or two per customer | Several, across departments and levels |
| Owner | An account manager with many accounts | A named key account manager with few accounts and a backup |
| Service | Standard terms and response times | Dedicated contact, prioritized service, sometimes tailored terms |
| Review | When something happens | Fixed rhythm, quarterly with the customer |

### What does a key account manager do?

A key account manager is the person responsible for the relationship with one or a few key accounts. The role usually covers five things:

- **Own the account plan** and keep it current.
- **Build relationships** with several people at the customer, not only the buyer.
- **Coordinate inside your company**, so delivery, support and finance act on what was promised.
- **Negotiate** contracts, renewals and extensions.
- **Report** on the account: revenue, risks, open actions.

Job titles vary by company. A sales manager or hunter mostly wins new customers in a market, while a key account manager grows and protects existing large ones. In a small team the same person, often the founder, does both, and the useful question is not which title is better but which accounts need KAM-level attention now. For finding new customers, see the [customer acquisition guide](/blog/customer-acquisition).

## How do you choose your key accounts?

Choose key accounts with a scoring table that you write down once and apply to every candidate. Four criteria cover most small B2B businesses, and the weights below are an example you should change to fit your business.

| Criterion | Weight | A 5 means | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue, last 12 months | x3 | In the top fifth of your customers by revenue | Your deals or invoices |
| Margin | x2 | Clearly above your average | Your own calculation, contribution margin where you know it |
| Growth potential | x3 | You can name concrete further needs, budgets or sites | Discovery calls and your notes |
| Strategic value | x2 | Reference customer, door to a market, or hard to replace | Your judgment, written down |

Rate each criterion from 1 to 5, multiply by the weight and add up. The maximum is 50. **Illustrative example (made-up numbers, not a benchmark):** a software consultancy rates six candidates.

| Candidate | Revenue (x3) | Margin (x2) | Growth (x3) | Strategic (x2) | Score | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manufacturer, 400 staff | 5 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 40 | Key account |
| Logistics company | 4 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 39 | Key account |
| Municipal utility | 3 | 5 | 2 | 5 | 35 | Key account |
| Medical group | 2 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 30 | Develop |
| Software start-up | 1 | 2 | 5 | 3 | 28 | Develop |
| Retail chain | 4 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 24 | Standard |

The cut-offs in this example are 35 or more for key account, 28 to 34 for develop and below 28 for standard. Three things show why the table is worth the effort. The retail chain brings the fourth highest revenue but scores lowest, because its margin and potential are low. The municipal utility is only third by revenue and still a key account, because margin and strategic value are high. The start-up is small today, so it goes on the develop list with a review in six months instead of a full account plan.

Four rules keep the list honest:

1. **Fix a maximum.** A number you can really keep in mind: a few accounts if the founder also sells, more with a dedicated key account manager.
2. **Write the cut-offs and weights down**, and allow an exception only with a written reason.
3. **Re-score once a year**, and let accounts move in and out.
4. **Watch concentration.** If one customer carries a large share of your revenue, list that as a risk in its plan even when the score is high.

If you already ranked customers by revenue with the ABC analysis in the [customer segmentation guide](/blog/customer-segmentation), the A class is your candidate list and the score decides which of them get KAM. Key partners such as a reseller can be key accounts too; the [sales channels guide](/blog/sales-channels) explains how partner and direct channels differ.

## What are the key account management processes?

There is no single standard list of five processes; textbooks and vendors divide the work differently. This guide uses five steps that cover the work from the first decision to the yearly reset:

1. **Select and tier** the accounts with the scoring table above.
2. **Plan:** write one account plan per key account.
3. **Map the stakeholders** and build relationships across the customer's organization.
4. **Run the rhythm:** weekly checks, monthly touchpoints, quarterly business reviews.
5. **Measure and adjust:** track a few numbers, re-score and update the plan every year.

## How do you write a key account plan?

An account plan is one page per account, short enough that it gets read. The table lists the sections, the question each one answers and an example for a made-up logistics company.

| Section | Question it answers | Example (made-up) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Snapshot | Who are they, what do we sell them, what share of our revenue? | Logistics company, 220 staff, two services, 11 percent of our revenue |
| Their goals | What does the customer want to achieve in the next 12 months? | Cut onboarding time at new depots, reduce the number of suppliers |
| Our goal | What do we want from the account, with a number and a date? | A third service live by September, framework agreement renewed in December |
| Opportunities | Where can we do more? | A second site, a service they currently buy elsewhere |
| Risks | What could shrink or lose the account? | Procurement reviewing suppliers, the sponsor changing roles |
| Stakeholders | Who matters, and who owns each relationship? | See the map below |
| Actions | What, who, by when? | Workshop with the new head of IT, key account manager, 15 November |
| Review | When do we look at the plan again? | Quarterly, next on 15 January |

Write the customer's goals before your own. A plan that only lists what you want to sell is a sales target, not an account plan.

## How do you map the stakeholders of a key account?

A stakeholder map lists the people at the customer who influence the relationship, what part each plays and how they feel about you. Its main purpose is to find the weak spot: an account where you know one person is one resignation away from losing its context.

| Person (made-up) | Role in the decision | Stance | Our relationship owner | Last real conversation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Head of operations | Decision maker | Supporter | Key account manager | 12 October |
| IT lead | Technical evaluator | Neutral | Solution engineer | 3 September |
| Procurement | Gatekeeper | Critical on price | Key account manager | 20 August |
| Dispatch team lead | Daily user | Supporter | Support lead | 29 October |

Three habits make the map useful. Know at least three people across two departments. Update it after every real conversation, not once a year. Give every person on it an owner on your side, so the relationship does not depend on one colleague. A "last real conversation" older than 90 days on a decision maker is the signal to act.

## How often should you review a key account?

Use a fixed rhythm, so reviews do not depend on someone remembering them.

| Rhythm | Who | What |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly, 15 minutes | Your team, internally | Which key accounts have no next step, no contact in 30 days or overdue actions |
| Monthly | The key account manager | One planned touchpoint per account that is not about a problem: share a result, an idea or an introduction |
| Quarterly | With the customer | The quarterly business review (QBR) |
| Yearly | Internally | Refresh the plans, re-score the tiers, decide who enters or leaves the list |

A quarterly business review needs an agenda you send in advance: results against the customer's goals, open issues and how they were solved, what is changing in their plans, opportunities, and the agreed actions with an owner and a date. Let the customer do most of the talking, end with no more than three dated actions, and do not turn it into a sales pitch. The same review habit sits in the [CRM strategy guide](/blog/crm-strategy), which covers retention and quarterly checks for the whole customer base.

## Which numbers show whether key account management works?

Track four to six numbers per quarter. More than that and nobody reads them.

| Number | How to calculate | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue per key account | Revenue of the last 12 months, compared with the 12 months before | Whether the account grows, holds or shrinks |
| Revenue concentration | Share of total revenue from all key accounts, and from the largest one | How exposed you are to a single customer |
| Renewal rate | Key accounts renewed divided by key accounts up for renewal | Whether the relationship holds when it is tested |
| Margin per account | Margin on that account's revenue, where you can calculate it | Whether growth is profitable |
| Breadth | Contacts per account with a real conversation in the last 90 days | Whether the account depends on one person |
| Review discipline | Key accounts with a current plan and a review in the last quarter, divided by all key accounts | Whether the process is actually run |

Revenue and margin need data from your invoicing or accounting; a CRM that tracks deals holds the part you recorded, so say which source each number comes from. What a customer does after signing, and why it matters for renewals, is part of the [customer journey](/blog/customer-journey).

## Which mistakes do teams make with key accounts?

- **Choosing by gut feeling or by revenue alone**, so the list fills up with large, low-margin customers.
- **Too many key accounts.** When everyone is key, nobody gets the attention.
- **A single contact.** One resignation or reorganization ends the relationship.
- **A plan written once** and never opened again.
- **A review that is a sales pitch**, so the customer stops coming.
- **No way out.** Accounts that no longer meet the criteria stay on the list because nobody re-scores.
- **Knowledge in one head.** If the history, agreements and notes are not on the record, the account leaves with the key account manager.
