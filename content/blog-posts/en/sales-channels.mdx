# What Is a Sales Channel? Direct, Indirect and How to Choose

What is a sales channel? It is the route through which a customer buys from you: your own sales team, your website, a reseller, a marketplace or a shop. Channels are either direct (you sell to the customer yourself) or indirect (a partner sells for you, which is what "channel sales" means), and most companies run a mix. Choose the ones that fit where your buyers already look, then record the channel on every deal so you can see which one actually produces customers.

This guide covers both meanings of the term, the types of sales channels with B2B and B2C examples, the real differences between direct sales and channel sales, a comparison by cost, control, speed and fit, a worked example for measuring channels, and a setup for tracking the channel on each deal. Customermates, the CRM that publishes this guide, can hold the channel on a deal and chart results by channel; it is not an attribution or e-commerce tool, and the last section says where a dedicated tool fits better.

## What is a sales channel?

A sales channel is the path by which a sale happens: who the customer deals with, and where. For a software vendor it can be the founder's own demo calls, a signup page or a reseller. For a manufacturer it is often a distributor and a retail shop. For a consultancy it is frequently referrals from other advisers.

Two things a sales channel is not:

- **Not a marketing channel.** A marketing channel (a LinkedIn post, a newsletter, search ads) earns attention. A sales channel is where the purchase is agreed. One buyer can meet you through three marketing channels and still buy through a single sales channel.
- **Not always a distribution channel.** For physical goods, the distribution channel describes how the goods travel (warehouse, wholesaler, shop), while the sales channel describes how the sale is made. For software and services the two are usually the same path.

### What is the difference between a sales channel and channel sales?

The phrase has two meanings, and search results mix them up.

- **Sales channel** is the general term: any route to the customer, direct or indirect.
- **Channel sales** is a sales model: selling through third parties such as resellers, distributors, agencies, system integrators or referral partners, who sell your product or service for a share of the margin or a fee. It is the same thing as indirect sales, so it is one family of sales channels and not a synonym for all of them.

E-commerce tools use the word more narrowly. Shopify, for example, lists its online store, point of sale, social commerce and marketplaces such as Google and Amazon as sales channels (checked on 7 November 2026 in [Shopify's help center on sales channels](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/online-sales-channels)). If you sell physical products online, that is the meaning behind most searches for "Shopify sales channels". The rest of this guide uses the broader B2B meaning.

## What are the types of sales channels?

There are two families, direct and indirect, and most companies combine them (a hybrid or multichannel model).

| | Direct: you sell | Indirect: a partner sells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B2B examples | Founder-led sales, inside sales, field sales, outbound email, LinkedIn and calls, demo requests on your website, self-serve signup, your own events | Resellers, distributors, agencies and integrators that implement your product, referral partners, app directories and marketplaces |
| B2C examples | Your own online shop, your own stores, orders by phone or mail, your own app | Retailers, wholesalers, online marketplaces, affiliates |
| Customer relationship | Yours | Shared with the partner, or the partner's |
| Price | You set it | You set it with the partner, who keeps a margin or fee |

A software vendor might sell to small customers through self-serve signup, to mid-sized customers through its own sales team and to customers in other countries through a local integrator. Each of those is a separate channel with its own cost, speed and follow-up, which is why the next sections compare them and track each one.

## What are the key differences between direct sales and channel sales?

Direct sales and channel sales differ in who does the selling, and therefore in who holds the relationship, the margin and the speed of growth.

| | Direct sales | Channel sales (indirect) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who sells | Your own employees | The partner's people, who usually sell other things too |
| Customer relationship | Yours | Shared, or the partner's unless the contract says otherwise |
| Margin | You keep the full price and carry the whole cost of selling | You give the partner a margin, discount or fee and carry less selling cost |
| Control | High: message, price, follow-up | Lower: the partner decides how much attention you get |
| Growth | Limited by your headcount | Wider reach once partners are recruited and trained, but a slow start |
| Customer feedback | First hand | Filtered through the partner |
| Fits when | Deals are complex, high value or relationship-led | You cannot reach the market alone, or buyers expect a local expert or an implementation partner |

### What is indirect sales?

Indirect sales means someone who is not your employee sells your product or service to the customer. Resellers buy and resell; referral partners introduce you and take a fee when the deal closes; integrators bundle your product into their own projects. Before you sign a partner, agree in writing:

1. **Who owns the customer record** and the data, and who does support and onboarding.
2. **How deals are registered**, so two partners (or a partner and you) do not chase the same account.
3. **What the partner earns** and when it is paid.
4. **What price floor or discount limits apply.**

A partner channel moves selling effort to the partner. It does not remove onboarding, support or the follow-up on each referred deal, so those still need an owner. Once a deal exists, it moves through your [sales pipeline stages](/blog/sales-pipeline) like any other.

## How do you choose the right sales channels?

Choose by where your buyers already look, buy or ask for advice, not by which channel looks most modern. Written down, that choice is your sales channel strategy: which channels, for which buyers, with which owner and which measure. The table compares the channels most small B2B teams consider. The ratings are our rule of thumb for a small team, not measured benchmarks, and your numbers will differ.

| Channel | Cost to run | Control | Speed to first deals | Fits when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Own outbound (email, LinkedIn, calls) | Medium: your time and tools | High | Fast, days to weeks | You can name the buyer and a reason to write; see the [cold calling guide](/blog/cold-calling) |
| Website and self-serve signup | Low to medium once built | High | Slow to start, needs traffic | The product can be bought without a conversation |
| Content and search | Medium, mostly writing time | High | Slowest, months | Buyers search for the problem you solve |
| Referral partners | Low: a fee or a favour in return | Medium | Medium | Advisers already serve your buyers and trust you |
| Resellers and distributors | Medium to high: margin, training, support | Low | Slow to start, then wide | The product needs local presence, bundling or implementation by someone else |
| Marketplaces and app directories | Medium: listing effort and platform fees | Low | Medium | Your buyers already shop there and you can live with the platform's rules |
| Events and trade fairs | High per event | High | Fast but short | Your market meets in person |

Five questions settle most decisions:

1. **Where do your buyers already look?** Go there before you build anything new.
2. **How big and complex is the deal?** High value and a long cycle favor a conversation-led direct channel. Low-touch purchases suit self-serve and marketplaces.
3. **Who follows up, and do you have the capacity?** A direct channel costs your hours, a partner channel costs support and enablement.
4. **What margin can you share?** Work out your price after the partner's share before you sign anyone.
5. **Which two channels can you test for a quarter?** One main channel that can produce conversations this month and one supporting channel that builds over time. Six channels at once usually means none gets proper follow-up.

## How do you measure which sales channel works?

Compare channels on five numbers, all per channel and per quarter:

- **Deals won** and **win rate** (won divided by won plus lost).
- **Cost per won deal:** everything spent on that channel (tools, fees, partner shares, the part of salaries spent on it) divided by the deals it won.
- **Average deal value.**
- **Sales cycle:** days from the deal being created to won.

**Illustrative example (made-up numbers, not a benchmark):** a small software consultancy tracks one quarter.

| Channel | Cost in the quarter | Deals won | Cost per won deal | Average deal value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Own outbound | 6,000 | 4 | 1,500 | 8,000 |
| Website and content | 3,000 | 2 | 1,500 | 6,000 |
| Referral partners | 2,400 | 3 | 800 | 9,000 |
| Events | 4,500 | 1 | 4,500 | 10,000 |
| **All channels** | **15,900** | **10** | **1,590** | **8,100** |

In this example the referral partners cost least per won deal and have the highest average deal value of the three channels that won more than one deal. The events channel looks expensive, but one deal is too small a sample to judge, so look at the next quarter before cutting it. Compare mature cohorts: deals created this month may not close this quarter.

Channels also overlap, because the buyer's path before the first conversation is part of the [customer journey](/blog/customer-journey). A buyer may read your content, receive an outbound email and then buy after a partner's introduction. Decide one rule and keep it, for example "the route through which the first conversation started", and write it down so everyone records the same way. A single channel per deal is a deliberate simplification, not attribution of every touchpoint.

## How do you track the sales channel per deal?

Four habits make channel data usable:

- **One field with a short fixed list**, five to eight options that do not overlap. An "Other" option only helps if you review it every month.
- **Set it when the deal is created**, not afterwards from memory.
- **Write the rule once** for edge cases, such as a partner introduction that you then work yourself.
- **Review monthly:** deals created, won and lost, value and cycle length per channel.
