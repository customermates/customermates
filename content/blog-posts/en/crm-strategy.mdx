# CRM Strategy: Seven Decisions That Fit on One Page

A CRM strategy is a short written plan that settles which customers you want to win and keep, which steps a relationship goes through, what data each step needs, who is responsible for the next move, and which numbers you look at every quarter. For a team of two to fifteen people it fits on one page. If yours runs to twenty, nobody will read it after the kickoff.

The software comes after those decisions. A CRM can store a stage, a date and an owner; it cannot decide for you what "qualified" means or when a renewal conversation starts.

Customermates publishes this guide, and the worked example below is set up in Customermates. The seven decisions work in any CRM.

## Strategy, system and the four types of CRM

The CRM system is the software. The strategy is the set of decisions the software has to support. Most failed rollouts skipped the second part: stages were copied from a vendor template, fields were added whenever someone asked, and six months later nobody trusts the pipeline.

Textbooks split CRM into four types. For a small team they are less a choice than a checklist of what the plan must cover:

| Type | What it covers | What it means for a small team |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational | Daily work: contacts, deals, tasks, follow-ups | Stages, next steps and who owns each deal |
| Analytical | Reading the data: segments, win rates, retention | Five or six numbers reviewed every quarter |
| Collaborative | Shared context across people and departments | One record per client that sales and delivery both read, including the conversations |
| Strategic | Long-term value of each relationship | Renewal dates, account health and expansion, tracked like deals |

A "CRM marketing strategy" is a narrower thing: using CRM data to plan campaigns and segments. Relationship-driven service businesses usually get more from the operational and collaborative rows first.

## The seven decisions of a CRM strategy

<Steps>
  <Step title="1. Set two or three goals with a number and a date">
"Better customer relationships" is not a goal. "Win rate on sent proposals from 25% to 35% within two quarters" is. So is "every active client has a renewal date and a health rating by the end of March". Write down today's value next to each goal. Without a baseline you cannot tell later whether anything changed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="2. Define who you serve, and who you don't">
Describe the clients you want more of in two or three sentences: size, industry, the problem they bring, the budget range. Then name the inquiries you will politely decline. This one paragraph decides what "qualified" means in step 3 and which fields you need in step 4.
  </Step>
  <Step title="3. Map the lifecycle, including after the signature">
List the stages a relationship goes through, each with an entry rule a colleague could check without asking you. A consulting example: Prospecting, Qualification, Discovery call, Proposal, Negotiation, Won, Lost. Then keep going past Won: onboarding, delivery, value review, renewal. Most CRM plans stop at the signature, which is where the recurring revenue starts. The [sales pipeline guide](/blog/sales-pipeline) covers stage design in more depth.
  </Step>
  <Step title="4. Decide the minimum data per record">
For each record type, pick the few fields that every record must have and the reason each one exists. Use fixed option lists instead of free text wherever you will filter or count later (source, industry, health). Fewer required fields means more complete records. Also decide how long you keep personal data and when you delete it: the GDPR's [Article 5](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) requires data to be limited to what is necessary and kept no longer than needed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="5. Write ownership and follow-up rules">
Every open deal has at least one assigned person and a dated next step. A deal without a next step is either lost or forgotten, and the weekly review should treat it as one or the other. Agree who owns the client after the signature, and what the handover to delivery must contain.
  </Step>
  <Step title="6. Bring conversations next to the records">
Write down where client conversations actually happen: email, LinkedIn, WhatsApp, calls. A strategy that assumes everyone logs every message by hand will fail in week three. Either connect those channels to the CRM or accept that the CRM only holds stages and summaries.
  </Step>
  <Step title="7. Fix a review rhythm">
Weekly: open deals without a future next step, and accounts rated red. Quarterly: the goals from step 1, the metrics below, and which fields nobody uses any more. Name one person who owns the CRM setup; on a small team that is often the founder.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Worked example: a six-person consultancy in Customermates

The following is an illustrative setup, not a customer case. A consultancy with four consultants and two people in delivery wants two things: fewer proposals that go quiet, and no renewal that arrives as a surprise.

**Deal phases.** A new Customermates workspace starts with a **Stage** column on deals (a Single Select column). The team edits its options to Prospecting, Qualification, Discovery call, Proposal, Negotiation, Won and Lost, and enters a win probability per option under **My Company › Settings**. Each deal then shows a weighted value: deal value times the stage weight. A deal's value is the sum of the services linked to it, so the team adds its standard offers as services. That is a fixed calculation from the percentages you enter, not a prediction. Details are in [the records documentation](/docs/app-records).

**Custom columns.** Customermates has ten column types, among them Date, Currency and Single Select. The team adds:

| Record type | Column | Type | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deals | Next step on | Date | Sort open deals by it in the weekly review |
| Deals | Source | Single Select | Referral, LinkedIn, Website, Event: count wins per source |
| Organizations | Renewal date | Date | Start the renewal conversation 90 to 120 days ahead |
| Organizations | Contract value | Currency | Revenue at stake per renewal |
| Organizations | Account health | Single Select | Green, Yellow, Red, with a written rule for each |

Tasks in Customermates have no built-in due date. If the team wants one, it adds a Date column to tasks and sorts by it.

**Saved views.** Each consultant saves the views they work from. Saved views are personal, so everyone creates their own.

- *My open deals*: deals assigned to me, Stage not Won or Lost, sorted by Next step on, oldest first.
- *Renewals*: organizations sorted by Renewal date, with Account health visible.
- *Pipeline board*: deals as a board grouped by Stage. Dragging a card changes the stage.

A dashboard chart shows weighted deal value grouped by Stage, and a second one counts organizations by Account health.

**Conversations.** The Pro plan includes [[commercial.entitlement.pro.includedAccountsPerUser]] connected account per user in the [unified inbox](/features/unified-inbox), and channels include email (Gmail, Outlook, IMAP), LinkedIn and WhatsApp. Participants are linked to contacts. A conversation stays private to the person whose account it came through until that person shares it with the team under **Thread settings**; the consultants share client threads so the delivery team can read the sales history before kickoff. Starter has no inbox.

**Two routines (Customermates Cloud).** Routines run Mate, the hosted assistant, with a saved instruction on a schedule or when a record changes. They use hosted AI credits from the owner's monthly allowance.

- Weekly, every Monday morning: *"List my open deals whose Next step on date has passed or is empty. For each, summarize the last conversation in one sentence and draft a follow-up reply. Do not send anything; leave drafts only."* The last sentence matters: Mate can send messages when asked, so a routine that should only draft has to say so.
- On a change: trigger **When something changes**, event **Deal Updated**, changed field Stage, record filter Stage is Won. Instruction: *"Create a task 'Kickoff' linked to this deal and its contacts, assigned to the deal's assigned users, and add a handover summary to the deal notes."*

A routine runs as its owner and sees only what that person can see. Limits and triggers are documented under [Routines](/docs/app-routines).

## Retention and customer success belong in the plan

Keeping clients is usually cheaper than winning new ones. In a 2014 Harvard Business Review article, Amy Gallo wrote that acquiring a new customer is five to 25 times more expensive than retaining one, and cited Bain & Company research by Frederick Reichheld that a 5% rise in retention raises profits by 25% to 95% ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)). The exact ratio depends on your business. The direction is why the lifecycle in step 3 does not end at Won.

For a small team, customer success management is not a department. It is a series of steps the account owner runs:

1. **Handover.** Sales writes down the client's goal, the success criterion and the people involved before delivery starts.
2. **Onboarding to first value.** Measure the days from signature to the moment the client reaches that success criterion.
3. **Value review.** Every quarter, or at a project milestone: what did the client get, what is next.
4. **Renewal.** Starts 90 to 120 days before the contract end, treated like a deal with its own stage.
5. **Expansion or referral.** A healthy account is asked for the next project or an introduction.

Write the health rule down. "Red: no meeting in 90 days, or the sponsor left, or an open complaint" is a rule two people will apply the same way. A gut feeling is not.

The numbers to review:

| Metric | How to calculate | Review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross revenue retention | Recurring revenue at the start of the period, minus churn and downgrades, divided by the starting revenue | Quarterly |
| Net revenue retention | Like gross retention, plus expansion revenue from the same clients | Quarterly |
| Client churn rate | Clients lost in the period divided by clients at the start | Quarterly |
| Time to first value | Days from signature to the agreed success criterion | Per new client |
| Win rate | Won deals divided by won plus lost deals in the period | Quarterly |
| Pipeline hygiene | Share of open deals with a next step date in the future | Weekly |

The first five measure results. The last one measures whether the CRM data can be trusted, and if it drops, the other numbers become fiction.

## Practices that keep a CRM strategy alive

- **Start with the smallest working setup.** Contacts, organizations, one deal pipeline. Add fields when a review shows you need them, not before.
- **Remove fields as well as adding them.** In the quarterly review, delete columns that nobody filled in.
- **Merge duplicates on import.** "Acme", "ACME GmbH" and "Acme Consulting" should be one organization before you migrate.
- **Migrate one record type at a time.** In Customermates, records import from XLSX workbooks, one record type per import: organizations first, then contacts, then deals.
- **Train on daily work, not on every feature.** An hour on the weekly review view beats a tour of the settings. The [CRM training guide](/blog/crm-training) has a session plan.
- **Do not automate a process you have not run by hand.** Run the weekly review manually for a month, then let a routine prepare it.

## Where Customermates fits, and where it does not

Customermates is an open-source, AI-native CRM for small client-facing teams such as consultancies and agencies. It suits the plan above when relationships run through email, LinkedIn and WhatsApp and you want them on the same record as the deal.

It is the wrong tool for parts of some strategies. It has no lead scoring, no data enrichment and no revenue forecasting beyond the stage weights you set. It has no email campaigns, sequences or bulk outreach, and no mobile app. Teams whose retention plan rests on product-usage data and automated health scores should look at a dedicated customer success platform. Teams whose strategy is mainly campaign marketing to large consumer lists need a marketing automation tool.

## FAQ

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What is a CRM strategy?">
A written plan for how your team wins, serves and keeps customers, and how the CRM records each step. It covers goals with numbers, target customers, lifecycle stages, the minimum data per record, ownership rules, the channels where conversations happen and a review rhythm. The software is chosen and configured to support those decisions.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
Operational CRM handles daily work such as contacts, deals and follow-ups. Analytical CRM reads the data, for example win rates and retention. Collaborative CRM shares context across people and departments. Strategic CRM focuses on the long-term value of each relationship. A small team's plan needs a piece of each.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the goals of a CRM strategy?">
Typical goals are more wins from the same number of inquiries, a shorter time to close, higher retention and data the team can trust. Each goal needs a number, a baseline and a date. Two or three such goals are enough.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is the best CRM strategy for a small business?">
The one your team will follow every week. In practice: two or three measurable goals, stages with clear entry rules, a dated next step on every open deal, renewal dates on every active client, and a fixed weekly review. Start small and add fields only when a review shows a gap. The [CRM implementation guide](/blog/crm-implementation) covers the rollout.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How does customer retention management fit into a CRM strategy?">
Retention is the part of the lifecycle after the signature: onboarding, value reviews, account health and renewals. In a CRM it needs a renewal date, a contract value and a health rating per client, a view sorted by renewal date, and quarterly metrics such as gross and net revenue retention.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are CRM best practices?">
Keep required fields few, use option lists instead of free text, merge duplicates before migration, train on daily workflows, and review the setup every quarter. Do not automate a process before you have run it by hand. Define how long you keep personal data, as the GDPR requires.
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
