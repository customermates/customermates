# CRM Training: What Each Role Must Learn and a 30-Day Rollout Plan

CRM training means teaching each person on your team to do their weekly work in the CRM: every role learns the five or six actions it repeats, practices them on real records in one session of 60 to 90 minutes just before go-live, and then reviews the pipeline from inside the CRM for 15 minutes a week during the first month. If you want CRM training for your own career instead, learn in the free academy of the CRM you will actually work in; the course section below compares the main ones.

Customermates publishes this guide. The plan works in any CRM. The Customermates section near the end says what our product offers for training and where it does not help, and the course section names other vendors' programs with links to their own pages.

## What should each role be able to do after CRM training?

Most CRM training fails for a dull reason: people were shown every screen once and never practiced the few things they do each week. Train by role instead, and let every role leave with one check it can pass in front of you. The session lengths below are a planning starting point for a team of two to fifteen people, not a measured benchmark.

| Role | Must be able to | Session | How you check it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Admin (owns the setup) | Add or rename a stage, add or hide a field, give each role the access it needs, import a client file and fix rejected rows, keep the one-page rules current | 90 minutes, after about 6 hours of preparation | Imports a 20-row test file, adds a stage and a field, and tests the result with a non-admin login |
| Manager or founder | Read the pipeline board, run the weekly review from it, filter to one person's open deals, spot deals without a next step | 60 minutes | Runs the first weekly review from the board with no spreadsheet open and ends it with every gap fixed or assigned |
| Sales rep | Find any contact or organization from search, create a deal from an inquiry, move it, set the next step, write a note a colleague could act on, save a "my open deals" view | 90 minutes | Updates five of their own real deals during the session; the trainer opens two and confirms note and next step are usable |
| Support or back office | Find a customer in seconds, read the history, log what was said or agreed, link it to the right records, hand a case over with a clear note | 60 minutes | Answers three real customer questions from the CRM record alone, without asking a colleague |

The written rules matter as much as the clicks. One page is enough: which stages exist and what has to be true to enter each one, which fields are required, where client emails belong, and what nobody should store in the CRM (private opinions about people, passwords, health data). If the rules are not written, each person invents their own and the data stops being comparable within weeks. The [CRM strategy guide](/blog/crm-strategy) covers how to decide those rules in the first place.

## What are the basic CRM skills?

Basic CRM skills are small and concrete. This checklist is the sign-off sheet behind the role table: by the end of the first month, every person ticks the groups that apply to their role.

**Find and read**
- Open the right contact, organization or deal from search in under ten seconds
- Read a record's history and say what happened last and what is planned next

**Write**
- Search before creating, so there is no second record for the same client
- Write a note a colleague could act on without asking you
- Put a next step with a date on every open deal

**Move deals**
- Move a deal to the next stage only when the written rule for that stage is true (the [sales pipeline guide](/blog/sales-pipeline) shows how to write them)
- Close a deal as won or lost on the same day, with the reason if your rules ask for one

**See your own work**
- Filter the deal list to your own open deals and save it as a view

**Keep the data honest**
- Update the record the same day after a client contact
- Know what must never be stored in the CRM

**Manager and admin extras**
- Manager: run the weekly review from the board and decide which fields to drop
- Admin: change stages and fields, set roles, import and correct a client file

## How do you roll out CRM training in 30 days?

This schedule assumes a team of two to fifteen people and a CRM that is already chosen. Day 1 is go-live, the first day people do real work in the CRM. If you are still choosing or migrating, the [CRM implementation guide](/blog/crm-implementation) comes first.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Before day 1: configure, load real data, write the rules (admin, about 6 hours)">
  The admin sets up stages, custom fields and roles, imports the current clients and open deals, and writes the one-page rules. Pick one or two people who like the tool and give them access a week early; they test the setup with a real deal and answer the small questions later.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Day 1 and the two days before: one session per role (60 to 90 minutes each)">
  Run the sessions from the role table no more than two or three days before people start using the CRM for real work. Groups of four to eight, so everyone has their hands on the keyboard. Each person works on their own deals, not on a fictional "Acme Corp". Send the rules page right after.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Days 2 to 7: questions go to the champions (30 minutes of office hours)">
  The one or two early users hold a fixed 30-minute slot for questions. On day 7 the manager opens the pipeline board in the weekly meeting and goes through it deal by deal, 15 minutes. Anything missing is fixed on the spot.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Days 8 to 21: weekly 15-minute reviews from the CRM (days 14 and 21)">
  The review is the training: people update the CRM because the meeting runs from it. The admin collects the complaints and removes one unused field or renames one confusing stage per week, so people see that feedback changes the setup.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Days 22 to 30: measure, help the stragglers, adjust">
  Run the adoption checks below. Give the one or two people who are behind a 20-minute one-to-one rather than another group session, update the rules page, and decide what to teach next, for example reports or the inbox.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### A 90-minute session for people who sell

| Minutes | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 10 | Why | What each person gets back: no more asking around for the last email to a client, no lost follow-ups. Not "management wants reports". |
| 10 to 30 | Records | Find a contact, open their organization, read the history, add a note |
| 30 to 55 | Deals | Create a deal from a real inquiry, move it through the [pipeline stages](/features/pipeline), set the next step |
| 55 to 70 | Their own view | Each person filters the deal list to their own open deals and saves it |
| 70 to 90 | Practice | Everyone updates five of their own real deals while the trainer walks around |

The manager session keeps the Records block short and replaces the deal practice with reading the board and running the weekly review. Support and back-office roles use the Records block and the own-view block, plus a practice round on real customer questions. The admin session covers stages, fields, roles and import.

### What this costs in time

For a five-person team (one admin, one manager, three reps), a realistic first month looks like this, counting every participant's time: about six hours of preparation for the admin, about seven person-hours of sessions (admin 90 minutes, manager 60, three reps 90 each), and four weekly reviews of 15 minutes with five people (5 person-hours). That is roughly 18 to 20 person-hours, a planning estimate rather than a benchmark. The preparation is the part people skip, and it decides whether the sessions use real data or a demo nobody cares about.

## How do you measure whether CRM training worked?

Logins and "activities per day" measure motion, not adoption: they rise when people click around and say nothing about whether the pipeline is true. Five checks that a team can read from its own filtered lists say more. Run them on day 30 and again on day 60.

| Check | How you read it | Healthy when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every open deal has an owner and a next step | Filter open deals for an empty owner or an empty next step | The list is empty, or every item has an explanation |
| Nothing sits untouched | Filter open deals by last update older than 14 days | A few deals, all parked on purpose; a long list means the work happens elsewhere |
| Five-conversation spot check | Take five client conversations from last week, from your sent mail or calendar, and look each one up in the CRM | All five have a note or a next step; two or fewer means the rules or the sessions need work |
| One record per client | Search ten client names | Each returns one organization and one contact, not duplicates |
| The weekly meeting runs from the board | Ask whether anyone opened a spreadsheet during the meeting | Nobody did, three weeks in a row |

The thresholds are rules of thumb, not benchmarks: adjust them to your team and watch the trend. If one person keeps showing up in the gaps, ask what is in the way. Often it is a field that makes no sense for their work, and the fix is to remove it. Measure the team's data, not individual keystrokes, and if your company has employee representation, agree beforehand which evaluations are allowed.

## Five mistakes that undo CRM training

1. **One session for everyone, covering every feature.** People remember the parts they used that week. Teach by role and leave the rest for later.
2. **Training weeks before launch.** By go-live the details are gone. Keep the gap to a few days.
3. **Practice data.** A fictional pipeline teaches the clicks but not the habit. Import your real clients first.
4. **The manager never opens it.** If the weekly meeting still runs from a spreadsheet, the CRM is optional, and everyone knows it.
5. **Too many required fields.** Each required field is a reason to update the CRM later, which often means never. Start with the fields the weekly meeting actually reads.

## Is CRM difficult to learn, and how long does it take?

Using a CRM is not hard, and [what a CRM is](/blog/what-is-crm) fits in a paragraph. The record types are contacts, organizations and deals, and most work is search, update and move. The daily basics take one session of 60 to 90 minutes for someone who sells. What is hard is the discipline of keeping it current: that is a team habit, built by running meetings from the CRM, and plan for about four weeks of weekly reviews before the team updates the CRM without being asked. Admin skills on a large platform take weeks of study; on a small-team CRM, an afternoon with stages, fields and import is usually enough.

## What are the 7 C's of a CRM, and what are the four pillars of CRM?

There is no single authoritative answer to either question, and we will not present one list as fact. The "7 C's of CRM" have no standards body behind them, and the lists differ by author: [WalkMe's article](https://www.walkme.com/blog/7-cs-of-customer-relationship-management/) names customer centricity, company culture, customer experience, customer data, customer journey, consumer experience and consumer expectations, while [Coffee's article](https://coffee.ai/articles/7-cs-of-crm-framework) gives two other seven-item variants and says the framework comes from practitioner writing and has no canonical version. The "four pillars of CRM" most often come out as people, strategy, processes and technology, as in [Flowlu's guide](https://www.flowlu.com/crm-guide/pillars-of-crm/), but other sources use different sets. If your course or exam uses one of these lists, learn that course's version. For a team, the parts you can train are people and process: who does what, and the written rules behind it, which is what the plan above covers.

## Learning CRM yourself: courses and certifications

If you want CRM skills for your own career, learn in the system you will work in. CRM software training from the vendor itself is free in most cases, and a Salesforce admin role needs Salesforce knowledge; general CRM theory helps less than an afternoon in the actual product. The main vendor programs:

| Program | What you get | Cost | Certification | Fits if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Salesforce Trailhead](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/what-is-trailhead/) | Self-paced modules and hands-on practice in Salesforce | Free learning platform | Salesforce certifications are separate exams; the fee is listed on each credential page | You aim for a Salesforce admin, developer or consultant role |
| [HubSpot Academy](https://academy.hubspot.com/courses) | Lessons and courses on the HubSpot CRM, sales and marketing | HubSpot says creating an account is free and opens its full library | [Free certifications](https://academy.hubspot.com/certification-overview) with a graded exam | Your company uses HubSpot, or you work in marketing |
| [Pipedrive Academy](https://learn.pipedrive.com/) | Courses, tutorials and webinars on Pipedrive | Free for all users, per Pipedrive | Completion certificates on some courses | You sell in a small team that uses Pipedrive |
| [Microsoft Dynamics 365 Fundamentals (CRM)](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/credentials/certifications/d365-fundamentals-customer-engagement-apps-crm/) | Exam on Dynamics 365 customer engagement apps | Microsoft marks this certification as retired | No longer available | Check Microsoft Learn for the current Dynamics 365 paths instead |

A certification is worth the time and any exam fee when you will configure a CRM for other people: as an admin, a consultant or in sales operations. For someone who sells or manages accounts, an hour of practice in your company's own setup does more than a certificate.

## How do you train a team on Customermates?

Customermates is an open-source, AI-native CRM for small client-facing teams. If your team uses it, a few parts of the product change how the plan above works in practice. This section describes what is in the product and what is not.

**Import before the sessions.** On Customermates Cloud, the [[commercial.trial.days]]-day trial lets you set up the workspace, import real data and run a first session before you pay. In each list, **More actions › Add from file** takes a CSV file, an Excel workbook (.xlsx or legacy .xls) or a JSON export of the same list, one list at a time: organizations first, then contacts, then deals. A wizard asks for the sheet, maps columns to fields and shows a preview, and rows that fail are listed with their reason. A file can be up to 10 MB and each sheet up to 5,000 data rows. The [records documentation](/docs/app-records) has the details.

**Write the rules where the tools read them.** The one-page rules belong in the workspace's [Knowledge Base](/features/knowledge-base). Put the standing rules in the Operating Guide and turn repeated tasks, such as "log a discovery call", into Procedures with a "When to use" line. Knowledge Base pages also work on self-hosted instances. Mate and connected AI clients read the Operating Guide first and follow a matching procedure before acting.

**Questions after the session: search, Mate and guided tours.** The documentation is reachable from the app: search (Cmd/Ctrl+K) lists matching documentation sections, each opening the place in the app it describes, and the avatar menu has **Documentation**. On Customermates Cloud, a new person can also open **Ask Mate** (Cmd/Ctrl+J), ask how something works and ask for a tour, for example "show me how to move a deal to the next stage and where my saved views are". Mate knows which page the person is on, builds a tour of 2 to 20 stops, moves between pages and explains each stop. It points at controls but does not click or type for them. This uses hosted AI credits from the monthly allowance each active user gets; see [pricing](/pricing) for the allowances. The [assistant documentation](/docs/app-assistant) lists what Mate can point at.

**Train each role on what that role sees.** Roles decide which lists a person can read, create and edit. With **Assigned** read access a person sees only the records assigned to them, and sidebar entries a role cannot open are hidden. Test the setup with a non-admin login before the sessions, so the screens you demonstrate are the ones people will have. The [workspace settings documentation](/docs/app-company) covers roles.

**Each person saves their own views.** A saved view in Customermates stays private until its owner shares it, so the "my open deals" step in the session still gives everyone their own list; a lead can also share one view with the team, read-only. The [records documentation](/docs/app-records) explains filters, grouping and the board.

**A next-step date, and the adoption checks as filters.** Customermates tasks have no due dates or reminders. For the "every deal has a next step" rule, add a custom Date field called "Next step on" to deals and sort your deal view by it. Two of the five checks then become filters: **Next step on** is empty, and the built-in **Updated at** field with **Older than…**, as described in the [filter reference](/docs/filter-syntax). A **Number** widget on the dashboard can show either count, and a chart grouped by **Assigned to** shows how many open deals each person holds. The **Activity** page lists record changes with who made them; use it to see whether records are moving, not to rank people.

**A weekly check as a Routine, in Customermates Cloud.** A manager can create a [routine](/docs/app-routines) that repeats every week on Monday at 8:00, with an instruction such as: "List the open deals assigned to me that have no Next step on date or were last updated more than 14 days ago. Write the list in the run summary. Do not send any messages." A routine runs as its owner and spends that person's hosted AI credits. Because Mate can send messages when an instruction asks it to, keep the "do not send" line in.

**Show first, practice second.** The public demo at [demo.customermates.com](https://demo.customermates.com/en/w/small-business/dashboard) is a read-only instance with sample data. You can open its lists, board and records to show people the screens, but nothing can be created or changed there and Mate does not answer in it. Practice happens in your own workspace, on your own records.

## FAQ

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What is CRM training?">
It is teaching people to use a customer relationship management system for their own work: finding records, keeping deals current, writing notes others can use, and following the team's rules for what goes where. In career terms, it also means courses and certifications that teach a specific CRM, such as Salesforce or HubSpot.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How long does it take to learn a CRM?">
The daily basics take one session of 60 to 90 minutes for someone who sells. The habit takes longer: plan for about four weeks of weekly reviews before the team updates the CRM without being asked. Admin skills on a large platform take weeks of study; on a small-team CRM, an afternoon with stages, fields and import is usually enough.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Can I learn CRM for free?">
Yes. Salesforce Trailhead, HubSpot Academy and Pipedrive Academy are free, and many CRMs offer a free plan or a trial you can practice in. Free courses teach the vendor's product; for general concepts, practice in one real system teaches more than theory.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does a CRM certification cost?">
It depends on the vendor. HubSpot Academy certifications are free. Salesforce certifications are separate exams, and the current fee is listed on each credential page on Trailhead. Microsoft has retired its Dynamics 365 Fundamentals (CRM) certification, so check Microsoft Learn for the current Dynamics 365 exams.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is CRM hard to learn?">
Using a CRM is not hard; most work is search, update and move. What is hard is keeping it current, and that is a team habit built by running meetings from the CRM, not by more training.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the 7 C's of a CRM?">
There is no authoritative list. Different articles publish different sets of seven, and Coffee's article says outright that no canonical version exists. If a course you take uses one list, learn that one for the exam; for a team, train the skills in the checklist above.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four pillars of CRM?">
The most common answer is people, strategy, processes and technology, but other sources name three or five pillars, so treat it as a teaching aid rather than a standard. For training, the pillars you control are people and process.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
The usual split is operational (daily sales and service work), analytical (reporting on customer data), collaborative (sharing customer context across teams) and strategic (customer-focused planning). Many sources list only the first three. The [overview of CRM systems](/blog/crm-systems) explains the types with examples.
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
