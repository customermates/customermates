# Existing Customers: Who They Are, Why They Matter and How to Look After Them

Existing customers are the people and companies that have already bought from you and that you have not closed as lost. For a small B2B team they are often the cheaper place to find the next sale and the first place where a quiet problem shows up, so they need their own routine: a written rule for who counts, the date of the last real conversation, a dated next step and a short weekly list of customers who have heard nothing from you for 60 days.

This guide defines the term with a table of edge cases, compares existing and new customers, puts the often-quoted cost and profit figures next to their source and date, lists six habits for looking after customers and walks through a Monday list with made-up customers. The last section sets the routine up in Customermates, the open-source CRM that publishes this guide, and says plainly what it leaves to you: it sends no reminders. Retention rate, cohorts and renewal planning belong to the [CRM strategy guide](/blog/crm-strategy); this page is about the everyday care of customers you already have.

## Who counts as an existing customer?

An existing customer, also called a current or repeat customer depending on the business, is a person or company that has bought from you and that you still treat as a customer. There is no universal threshold: how often your customers normally need you decides it. A web agency with yearly retainers and a shop with weekly orders need different windows. Write your rule down once, so two people sorting the same list reach the same answer, and put the status on the company, with its people linked to it.

| Situation | Existing customer? | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bought once, delivered and paid | Yes | Active |
| Active contract, retainer or subscription | Yes | Active |
| Signed, but not yet delivered or paid | Yes, treat it as onboarding | Active, with a first-delivery step |
| Last purchase older than your window, nothing planned | Not active, but no stranger | Dormant |
| Quote sent, nothing signed | No, a prospect | Stays a deal, see the [sales pipeline guide](/blog/sales-pipeline) |
| Contract ended, or you ended the relationship | Former customer | Former, with the reason in the notes |
| Your contact left the customer's company | Yes, the company is still the customer | Active, find the new contact |

The dormant row is the one most teams skip. A customer who bought two years ago and has not heard from you since is neither a prospect nor an active customer, and a separate status keeps that group out of the weekly list without forgetting it.

## What is the difference between existing and new customers?

| Question | New customers | Existing customers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What do you know? | Little: needs, budget and decision makers are assumptions | History, what they bought, who decided, what went well or badly |
| Trust | Built from nothing, every claim needs proof | Earned, and easy to lose through silence or a missed promise |
| Typical goal | A first order | A healthy relationship, the next order, an introduction |
| First step | Research, outreach, a discovery call (see the [customer acquisition guide](/blog/customer-acquisition)) | A check-in that is not a pitch |
| Main risk | No reply | Silence you do not notice until a competitor or a budget cut ends it |
| Where the data lives | Lead lists and the pipeline | The customer's record: people, notes, files, deals, conversations |

Neither replaces the other. A business without new customers shrinks as some leave, and a business that only chases new ones pays to replace customers it could have kept. For a small team the practical question is how much of the week each deserves. A simple start is a fixed slot, for example 30 minutes on Monday, for existing customers, before the new pipeline gets the rest of the week.

## Why do existing customers matter?

Two figures appear in almost every article on this topic. Both come from a Harvard Business Review article by Amy Gallo, published on 29 October 2014. It says that, depending on which study you believe and what industry you are in, acquiring a new customer is anywhere from five to 25 times more expensive than retaining an existing one. Citing research by Frederick Reichheld of Bain & Company, it also says that increasing customer retention rates by 5 percent increases profits by 25 to 95 percent. Checked on 11 November 2026; the source is at the end.

Three ways to read those numbers without overclaiming:

- **The ranges are wide on purpose.** The article itself ties the first one to the study and the industry. They are a reason to measure, not a forecast for your company.
- **They describe economics, not appetite.** A customer who has everything they need from you is a reference and a source of introductions, not an upsell target.
- **Your own records answer it better.** How many of last year's orders came from customers who had bought before? How many customers have not heard from you in 60 days? How many new customers were introduced by an existing one? Count these three once from your invoices or deals and you know where your time pays off.

## How do you look after existing customers?

Six habits cover most of it. None needs a tool, but each needs a date or a name that somebody keeps current.

1. **Give every customer an owner.** One named person, so "someone will call" does not mean nobody does.
2. **Record the last real conversation.** A date and one line on what was discussed or promised. "Real" means a two-way exchange, not a newsletter or an automatic invoice mail.
3. **Always leave a dated next step.** A call, a check-in message or a renewal date. A customer without a next step is left to chance.
4. **Check in without selling.** Ask how the delivered work is going, share a result or something useful, then stop. A check-in that ends in a pitch teaches the customer to ignore the next one.
5. **Ask at the natural moments.** After a delivery, after praise and at renewal, ask what comes next and whether they know someone with the same need.
6. **Treat silence as the signal.** A customer who has not heard from you for a set time goes on a list that you read every week. The worked example below shows one.

How often to check in depends on the customer. The rhythms below are starting points, not standards: set them by how often a customer normally needs you and adjust after a quarter. The groups can come from the [customer segmentation guide](/blog/customer-segmentation).

| Customer group | Check-in rhythm | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A handful of the most important customers | A planned touchpoint each month, a review each quarter | Run them like key accounts, see the [key account management guide](/blog/key-account-management) |
| Regular customers | A real conversation every two to three months | The weekly list catches the ones that slip |
| Small or one-off customers | Twice a year, or when a project ends | If nothing comes of it after a year, mark them Dormant |

## Worked example: a Monday list of customers gone quiet

**Illustrative example with made-up customers, not a customer case.** A five-person consultancy has 30 customers. On Monday 9 November its saved view lists Active customers whose Last contact is older than 60 days, oldest first. Five show up.

| Customer (made up) | Last contact | Next step on | Decision on Monday |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Logistics company | 12 August | none | The contact left. Find the new person through the organization's other people, then send a short message |
| Design studio | 17 July | none | One project last year. Set the status to Dormant and Next step on to 15 January |
| Food importer | 30 August | none | Call this week and ask about the follow-up project from the August call |
| Dental group | 28 August | 20 November | Nothing to do now, the step is set. It stays on the list until the call happens and Last contact is updated |
| Law firm | 3 September | none | Send a short check-in; if there is no reply in two weeks, call |

Three things show why the list works. Five names fit into one Monday slot; if the list showed fifteen, either the threshold is shorter than your customers' real rhythm or follow-up has slipped, and both are worth knowing. The dental group stays on the list although a step is planned, on purpose: the date of the last conversation, not the plan, measures silence. And every row ends in one of three decisions: contact, set a dated step, or move to Dormant. A list that never gets shorter means something is wrong.

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What are existing customers?">
Existing customers are people or companies that have already bought from you and that you have not closed as lost. No threshold fits every business, so write down your own window, for example a purchase or an active contract in the last 12 months, and record customers outside it as dormant.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is the difference between an existing customer and a new customer?">
A new customer has not bought from you yet or has just made a first order, so you work with assumptions. With an existing customer you hold history: what they bought, who decided and what you promised. The goal shifts from winning a first order to keeping the relationship healthy and finding the next need.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is it really cheaper to keep an existing customer than to win a new one?">
A Harvard Business Review article of 29 October 2014 reports a range of five to 25 times, depending on the study and the industry. That is a wide range from an older article, so treat it as a direction. Count how many of last year's orders came from customers who had bought before to see what it means for you.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How often should you contact existing customers?">
There is no standard. A starting point is a planned touchpoint each month and a review each quarter for a handful of top customers, a real conversation every two to three months for regular customers and twice a year for small or one-off customers. Adjust to how often a customer normally needs you.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How do you find existing customers who have gone quiet?">
Keep the date of the last real conversation on every customer, then list the customers whose date is older than your threshold, oldest first. In Customermates this is a saved view on Organizations with Customer status Active and Last contact Older than 60 days.
</FaqItem>

</FaqItem>
