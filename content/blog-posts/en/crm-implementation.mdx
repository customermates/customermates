# CRM Implementation: A Six-Step Plan for Small Teams

CRM implementation is the work between buying a CRM and the day your team opens it before anything else. For a team of up to about 20 people on a self-serve CRM, plan four weeks: one week to agree what the CRM must answer and how deals move, one to model and import the data, one to connect channels and train, and two weeks of daily use with one named owner fixing what breaks. The software settings take hours. The agreements and the data clean-up take the rest.

Customermates publishes this guide, and the worked example near the end uses Customermates. The plan itself works with any CRM.

## The plan in six steps

Every step ends with something you can point at. If a step produces no written output, it has not happened yet.

| Step | Output | Owner | Typical time, 5 to 15 people |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Define what the CRM must answer | A list of 5 to 10 questions, each with the field or view that answers it | Whoever runs sales, with the founder | 2 to 3 hours |
| 2. Model records and the pipeline | Record types, stage list with entry rules, 5 to 10 custom fields | CRM owner | Half a working day |
| 3. Clean and import data | One cleaned workbook per record type, imported in order | CRM owner plus one helper | 2 to 4 working days |
| 4. Connect channels and integrations | Mailboxes and other channels connected, at most two integrations live | CRM owner | 1 to 2 hours per channel and integration |
| 5. Roles, training, launch | Roles assigned, one 60 to 90 minute session on real deals, a launch date | CRM owner | 1 working day |
| 6. Review after two weeks | A short list of fields to drop, stages to rename, views to add | CRM owner with the team | 1 hour |

These are planning figures for a self-serve CRM, not measurements. A CRM project with several departments, an ERP connection or tens of thousands of messy records needs its own estimate.

### Step 1: Define what the CRM must answer

Skip the feature wishlist. Write the questions you want answered on a Monday morning, then name the field or view that answers each one:

- Which open deals have no next step agreed? A "Next step" date field, and a view filtered on empty or past dates.
- What is in the pipeline this quarter, and what is it worth if stages are weighted? A stage field with a probability per stage.
- Where did our last 20 clients come from? A "Source" single-select field.
- Who talked to this client last, and on which channel? The activity timeline, or a connected inbox.

Anything that answers none of your questions stays out of the first version. This one rule prevents most of the scope creep that sinks CRM projects. If you have not chosen a system yet, use the same list to compare candidates; the [guide to choosing a CRM](/blog/how-to-choose-crm) turns it into a scorecard.

### Step 2: Model records and the pipeline

Decide what a deal is in your business. For an agency it may be a project, for a recruiter a placement, for a consultancy a mandate. Then write the stages, each with a rule for entering it, so two people put the same deal in the same column:

| Stage | Enter when | Example probability |
|---|---|---|
| Qualification | First call booked, budget and need not yet confirmed | 10% |
| Demo or workshop | A meeting with the decision maker happened | 25% |
| Proposal | A written proposal was sent | 50% |
| Negotiation | The client asked for changes to scope, price or terms | 70% |
| Won | Signed | 100% |
| Lost | Declined, or no answer after the agreed last follow-up | 0% |

Five to seven stages are enough. Probabilities are your own estimate per stage; they turn deal values into a weighted pipeline, not a prediction. Add a "Lost reason" single-select early, because it is the field people regret not having after six months.

### Step 3: Clean and import data

Most teams start from spreadsheets, an old CRM export and a few mailboxes. Clean before you import, never after:

1. Export everything into one workbook per record type.
2. Delete records nobody has touched in three years unless they are clients.
3. Merge duplicates. Sort by email address and by company name; most duplicates sit next to each other.
4. Make values consistent: one spelling per country, one format per phone number, one value per dropdown option.
5. Import in dependency order: what you sell, then organizations, then contacts, then deals. A deal row that names an organization can then be linked to it.
6. Import ten rows first, check them in the CRM, then import the rest.

If your data still lives in a sheet, the [Excel CRM template](/blog/excel-crm-template) shows a layout that imports without retyping.

### Step 4: Connect channels and integrations

Connect the mailboxes and messaging accounts where client conversations already happen, so the CRM shows them without anyone copying text across. Limit integrations to two at launch, usually email and one system you cannot work without, such as accounting or a form tool. Every further integration is a separate small project after the two-week review.

For EU teams, data protection belongs in this step. A cloud CRM processes personal data on your behalf, and [Article 28(3) GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) requires a contract with the processor that sets out the subject matter, duration, nature and purpose of the processing. Sign the vendor's data processing agreement before you import real client data.

### Step 5: Roles, training, launch

Give each person a role that matches what they need to see. A common small-team setup: founders and the CRM owner see everything, everyone else sees all clients but only the deals assigned to them. Keep it simple at launch; tight permissions mostly produce "I can't find it" messages.

Train on your own deals, not a demo. One session of 60 to 90 minutes is enough when everyone moves their own five open deals through the stages during the session. Pick a launch date after which the old spreadsheet becomes read-only. Without that date, two systems run in parallel and the CRM loses. The [CRM training guide](/blog/crm-training) has a session outline.

In Germany, check one thing before launch: under [§ 87 (1) no. 6 BetrVG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/betrvg/__87.html), a works council has a say in introducing technical systems designed to monitor employee behaviour or performance. Activity histories and per-person pipeline views can fall under that. If you have a works council, involve it in step 1.

### Step 6: Review after two weeks

Look at what people actually filled in. Drop fields that stayed empty, rename stages people kept confusing, and add the two or three views that people asked for. Then decide on the next integration. This review is the step most teams skip, and it is where a CRM gets trimmed to the way the team works.

## CRM implementation checklist

**Before configuration**
- One named CRM owner with time set aside for four weeks
- 5 to 10 questions the CRM must answer, each mapped to a field or view
- Stage list with an entry rule per stage, plus a Lost reason field
- Works council involved, if you have one
- Data processing agreement signed with the vendor

**Data**
- One workbook per record type, duplicates merged, values made consistent
- Test import of ten rows checked by hand
- Full import in order: offerings, organizations, contacts, deals

**Launch**
- Mailboxes and channels connected for everyone who talks to clients
- At most two integrations live
- Roles assigned and tested with a non-admin account
- One training session on real deals
- Old spreadsheet set to read-only on launch day

**After two weeks**
- Empty fields removed, confusing stages renamed
- Missing views added
- Next integration chosen, or consciously postponed

## Do you need a CRM consultant?

A CRM consultant plans, configures, migrates and trains for you. On a large suite that is often money well spent. On a small team with a self-serve CRM it mostly buys the six steps above, done by someone who does not know your clients.

| Hire a consultant or partner if | Do it yourself if |
|---|---|
| You are rolling out a large suite with many modules | You use a self-serve CRM with standard objects |
| More than about 50 users across several departments | Under about 20 users with one sales process |
| ERP, telephony or data warehouse integration is in scope | Email plus one or two integrations cover it |
| Regulated data shapes the architecture | Standard GDPR obligations apply |
| Nobody internal can own the project for a month | One person can spend a few hours a day on it |

Some vendors build onboarding into the price. HubSpot's pricing page lists a required one-time onboarding fee of $1,500 for Sales Hub Professional and $3,500 for Enterprise, on top of the subscription. Both HubSpot and Salesforce run partner directories: the [HubSpot Solutions Directory](https://ecosystem.hubspot.com/marketplace/solutions), where partners earn tiers based on client results and can hold a CRM Implementation accreditation, and [AppExchange consultants](https://appexchange.salesforce.com/consulting) for Salesforce.

There is no reliable public benchmark for hourly or daily consulting rates, so get two proposals and compare what they deliver. A good proposal names a written output for each phase: a requirements document and stage definitions after planning, a configured test system and a migration plan after the build, and trained users plus a handover document after launch. Be careful with these patterns:

- Pure time and materials with no cap, especially for the planning phase.
- A system recommendation before anyone has talked to the people who sell.
- "We will refine requirements during the build."
- No data migration in scope, which returns later as a change request.
- No handover plan, so the team cannot change a field without calling the partner.
- An undisclosed reseller relationship with the CRM vendor. Ask directly.

If you are unsure, run the first two steps of this plan internally before asking for proposals. Either you find you can finish it yourself, or you get a far sharper brief.

## Why implementations stall

The pattern is consistent: the problem is rarely the software.

- **Too much at once.** Marketing automation, service desk and ERP sync all in phase one. Start with sales and client conversations only.
- **No owner.** The project sits with "everyone", so stage definitions are never written and nobody fixes the first annoyances.
- **Dirty data imported as is.** People find three versions of the same client on day one and go back to their spreadsheet.
- **Updating the CRM is extra work.** If people must copy emails and messages in by hand, entries stop after a few weeks. Connect the conversations instead.

## Worked example: implementing Customermates for a six-person consultancy

Here is the plan applied to Customermates, an open-source, AI-native CRM built in Germany, for a consultancy with six people who all talk to clients by email and LinkedIn. Every step below matches the current product; details are in the [records documentation](/docs/app-records) and the [company settings documentation](/docs/app-company).

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sign up and run the onboarding wizard">
    The wizard has four steps: Your profile (the only required one), Knowledge Base, Invite your team and Connect your AI. Invited people join as "Waiting for approval" until an admin assigns a role and sets them active under My Company › Members.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Name the record types and the pipeline">
    Under My Company › Settings, the Data model lets you rename Deals to Projects, for example. A new workspace starts with a Stage single-select on deals (Prospecting, Qualification, Demo, Proposal, Negotiation, Won, Lost). Edit the options to match your stage table, pick it as the Deal stage field and enter a probability per stage to get a weighted pipeline.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add the fields from step 1">
    Add custom columns: "Next step" as a date on deals, "Source" and "Lost reason" as single-select. Customermates has no tags on records; single-select fields cover that job and work for grouping on the board.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Import one record type at a time">
    Use Import and export › Add from file on each list. It reads .xlsx workbooks, not CSV, and lets you map columns to fields; a Record ID column updates existing records instead of creating duplicates. Import services, then organizations, contacts and deals. Row and file size limits are in the records documentation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set roles">
    Keep the built-in Admin role for the two partners. Create one custom role for consultants: Read access All on contacts and organizations, Manage Yes with Read access Assigned on deals, so each person sees and edits only the deals they are assigned to.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Connect inboxes and save views">
    On Pro, each person connects one account in the unified inbox, usually their mailbox, so client conversations sit next to the contact. Connecting LinkedIn as well is a second account per person, which needs Business. Each person saves their own view, for example "My open deals, Next step empty or past". Saved views are personal, so this happens once per person during training.
  </Step>
</Steps>

On Customermates Cloud, a routine can take over the Monday question from step 1. Routines run Mate, the in-app assistant, on a schedule and use hosted AI credits. An example instruction for a weekly routine every Monday at 8:00: "List my open deals where Next step is empty or in the past, with the last message from each contact. Do not send anything and do not change records." A routine runs as its owner and may send messages without asking, so an instruction that should only report must say so, as this one does. See the [routines documentation](/docs/app-routines) for triggers and limits.

**What this costs.** For six people on Pro, which includes the [unified inbox](/features/unified-inbox) with [[commercial.entitlement.pro.includedAccountsPerUser]] connected account per user and [[commercial.entitlement.pro.includedRoutinesPerUser]] routines per user, the subscription is [[commercial.price.pro.monthly.seats.6]] per month, billed monthly. If every consultant wants both their mailbox and LinkedIn in the inbox, Business includes [[commercial.entitlement.business.includedAccountsPerUser]] connected accounts per user and costs [[commercial.price.business.monthly.seats.6]] per month for six people. Starter costs [[commercial.price.starter.monthly.seats.6]] per month for six people but has no inbox. Starter, Pro and Business include monthly hosted AI credits per active user; amounts are on the [pricing page](/pricing). There is a [[commercial.trial.days]]-day trial, enough to run steps 1 to 3 on your real data.

<AcquisitionCallout title="Where Customermates is the wrong fit" variant="boundary">
  Customermates has no task due dates or reminders (a custom date field and a filtered view cover follow-ups), no email sequences, no mobile app, no shared team views and no field-level permissions. Import and export are XLSX only, one record type at a time. The self-hosted edition is the open-source core with REST, webhooks and MCP, but without the inbox, Mate or routines. If you need marketing automation, telephony or an ERP-grade suite, look at a larger platform and budget for a partner.

## FAQ

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What is CRM implementation?">
CRM implementation is the process of getting a CRM into daily use: defining what it must answer, modelling records and pipeline stages, cleaning and importing data, connecting email and other channels, setting roles, training the team, and reviewing the setup after launch. Choosing the system usually comes just before it.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How long does CRM implementation take?">
For a small team on a self-serve CRM, about four weeks, of which two are live use with fixes. Salesforce's own guide says small businesses can need just a few weeks, while larger enterprises with complex integrations may need several months. Data clean-up decides the timeline more than configuration does.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What should a CRM implementation plan include?">
For each step, an output, an owner and a date: the questions the CRM must answer, the stage list with entry rules, the field list, the import order, connected channels and integrations, roles, the training session, the launch date when the old system turns read-only, and a review date two weeks later. The six-step table above is a usable starting point.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the phases of CRM implementation?">
Guides divide it differently: Salesforce lists nine steps, Pipedrive five, consultants often use plan, build and run. The content is the same: requirements, configuration, data migration, training and launch, then review. What matters is that each phase ends with a written output.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does CRM implementation cost?">
The subscription is the visible part. Internal time for clean-up, configuration and training is usually larger for small teams. Some vendors add a required onboarding fee, for example $1,500 for HubSpot Sales Hub Professional. A consultant-led project adds day rates on top. The [CRM cost guide](/blog/crm-cost) breaks down the full calculation.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is a CRM hard to learn?">
The basics of a modern CRM take an hour: find a contact, move a deal, log a note. What takes longer is the agreement on how your team uses it, which is why the stage definitions and a training session on real deals matter more than the tool.
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
