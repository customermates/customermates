# CRM Examples: Seven Real Workflows, the Software Behind Them and What They Cost

The clearest CRM examples are workflows, not logos: a freelancer who checks every Monday which proposals are still open, an agency where a colleague can answer a client's email because the whole thread sits on the client record, and a sales team that moves each deal through fixed stages and reads its forecast from a dashboard. Well-known examples of CRM software behind those workflows are Salesforce Sales Cloud, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Sales, Zoho CRM and Pipedrive, plus open-source systems such as SuiteCRM and Customermates.

This guide starts with seven workflows by team size and CRM type, then names the software, answers whether Excel counts as a CRM, and lists what CRM tools cost per user. Customermates publishes it and appears in its own labelled section. Vendor facts were checked on the vendors' own pages on the dates given next to them. If you need the basics first, start with [what a CRM is](/blog/what-is-crm).

## CRM workflow examples by team size

Each example below names the team, what lands in the CRM, the weekly routine that keeps it useful, and the type of CRM work it is. The names are invented; the patterns are the ones that show up in almost every small and mid-sized setup.

| Team | Size | Main records | The workflow in one line | CRM type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Freelance consultant | 1 | Contacts, deals | Every inquiry becomes a deal; Monday review of open proposals | Operational |
| Marketing or design agency | 3 to 10 | Organizations, contacts, deals, email threads | Client emails sit on the client record, so anyone can answer | Collaborative |
| B2B sales team | 10 to 50 | Organizations, contacts, deals with stages | Fixed stages, weighted pipeline, weekly forecast meeting | Operational and analytical |
| Recruiting agency | 2 to 20 | Candidates, clients, open roles | Each role is a deal with many candidates attached | Operational |
| Property agent | 1 to 5 | Buyers, sellers, properties | Two sides per deal, viewings and offers logged by date | Operational |
| Support desk | 3 to 30 | Customers, tickets, conversation history | Agent opens the full history before replying | Collaborative |
| Enterprise sales and marketing | 200+ | Accounts, opportunities, campaigns | One customer view across regions and departments | Analytical and strategic |

### Solo consultant: one inbox, one pipeline

Anna runs a one-person consulting business. Every inquiry, whether it arrives by email, LinkedIn or a referral, becomes a deal with a value and a stage: Inquiry, Call held, Proposal sent, Won, Lost. On Monday morning she filters the deals in Proposal sent that have not changed for ten days and writes a follow-up for each. Without a CRM, those proposals live in her sent folder and are forgotten the week a big project starts. The [sales follow-up email templates](/blog/sales-follow-up-email) show what she writes.

### Agency of six: shared client history

At a six-person design agency, the account lead goes on holiday in the middle of a pitch. Because the client's emails, the call notes and the proposal sit on the client's record, a colleague reads the thread and replies the same day. This is the collaborative side of CRM: the history belongs to the company, not to one person's mailbox. The agency also records which services each client has bought, so the next proposal starts from real numbers.

### B2B sales team of twenty: stages and a forecast

A software company with twenty sellers defines seven stages, from Prospecting to Won and Lost, each with a win probability. Every Friday, the sales lead opens a dashboard that sums deal value by stage and by seller, and the team discusses only the deals that have not moved. That dashboard is the analytical side of CRM. The rules for the stages are in the [sales pipeline guide](/blog/sales-pipeline).

### Recruiting agency: many people per deal

A recruiting agency treats each open role as the deal and attaches the candidates and the client's hiring manager to it as contacts. The stages are Sourced, Screened, Interview, Offer and Placed. Most conversations happen on LinkedIn, so the agency wants those messages on the candidate's record instead of in a browser tab.

### Property agent: two sides of every deal

An estate agent links one property to a seller and several interested buyers. Viewings, offers and the notary date are logged with dates, so the agent sees at a glance which buyer is waiting for an answer. The work is phone-heavy, so the CRM either logs calls by hand or connects to a telephone system.

### Support desk: history before the reply

A support team of eight opens the customer's record before answering a ticket: what they bought, what they asked last time, who promised what. Help-desk tools such as ticket queues with service-level timers are a separate category; many CRM suites sell them as an add-on.

### Enterprise: one customer view across departments

Large organizations use CRM to connect sales, service and marketing. Salesforce writes that Siemens put 18,000 sellers, resellers and partners on a single CRM across seven business units, and that FedEx uses Marketing Cloud to flag quotes that are not followed by a shipment ([Salesforce CRM examples](https://www.salesforce.com/crm/examples/)). These are vendor case studies, not independent results. What transfers to a small team is the pattern: one record per customer, and no switching between tools to answer a question.

## CRM examples by type: operational, analytical, collaborative

Textbooks split CRM into operational, analytical and collaborative CRM, and some add strategic CRM as a fourth. Most products cover several types at once, so the split helps you name a need, not pick a product. For the longer explanation, see [CRM systems and their types](/blog/crm-systems).

| Type | What it does | Concrete example | Typical software |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Operational | Runs the daily work: records, pipeline, tasks, messages | A rep moves a deal from Proposal to Negotiation and logs the call in the deal's notes | Pipedrive, Zoho CRM, Customermates |
| Analytical | Turns the records into numbers | A dashboard shows won deal value by lead source for the last quarter | Salesforce, Dynamics 365, HubSpot reports |
| Collaborative | Shares one customer history across people and teams | A colleague covering a holiday reads the full email thread before replying to the client | HubSpot, Customermates (threads shared to the CRM) |
| Strategic | Aligns the business around long-term customer value | A firm reviews which clients bring repeat work and plans account time around them | Any CRM with clean data and reports |

A marketing CRM is a further label you will see: tools such as HubSpot's Marketing Hub or Salesforce Marketing Cloud segment contacts and send campaigns from the same customer database. A collaborative CRM example in a small business is simply a shared client record that the whole team reads before replying.

## Is Excel a CRM?

No. Excel is a spreadsheet: it can hold a contact list and a simple deal sheet, and many small teams start there. It is not a CRM because it does not log the emails you send, does not remind anyone of a next step, and does not keep one history per customer when several people edit the same file. Two people sorting the same sheet at once, or a colleague saving a copy on their laptop, is usually the moment a team moves on.

| Need | Excel or Google Sheets | CRM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact list with company and phone | Yes | Yes |
| Deal list with value and stage | Yes, by hand | Yes, with board and weighted totals |
| Email and message history per customer | No | Yes, if the CRM connects your mailbox |
| Reminder when a deal goes quiet | No, unless you build formulas | Yes, through views, tasks or automation |
| Several people editing safely | Limited | Yes, with roles and an activity log |

A good test: if you have fewer than about 50 active contacts and work alone, a workbook is fine. [The Excel CRM template guide](/blog/excel-crm-template) gives you a ready layout and shows when to switch.

## A worked setup: a four-person consultancy in Customermates

Here is the agency-style workflow taken all the way down to the columns. It uses the real lists and labels of Customermates; you can rebuild it in any CRM that has custom fields and saved filters.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Set up the lists">Clients go in **Organizations**, people in **Contacts**, and each engagement you are trying to win in **Deals**. Your standard offers go in **Services** with a price each (for example "Strategy workshop" and "Monthly retainer"), and a deal's value follows from its line items, each a service and a quantity.</Step>
  <Step title="Rename the deal stages">The deal **Stage** is a Single choice field. Replace the defaults with Inquiry, Discovery call, Proposal sent, Won and Lost. Each option carries a probability, so every deal gets a weighted value next to its value.</Step>
  <Step title="Add three custom fields">On deals: "Engagement type" (Single choice: Project, Retainer, Workshop), "Lead source" (Single choice: Referral, LinkedIn, Website, Event) and "Expected start" (Date). Customermates has no tags; Single choice fields do that job and can be filtered and grouped.</Step>
  <Step title="Save a personal view">On **Deals**, filter Stage = Proposal sent and assigned users = you, sort by updated date and save it with **New view** as "My open proposals". Saved views are personal, so each colleague sets the same filters and saves their own.</Step>
  <Step title="Import existing clients">Export your client list to an Excel workbook (.xlsx) and import organizations first, then contacts, one list at a time. See [records, import and views](/docs/app-records) for the column matching.</Step>
  <Step title="Connect the inbox (Pro or higher)">Each consultant connects their mailbox or LinkedIn account in the [unified inbox](/features/unified-inbox). Pro includes [[commercial.entitlement.pro.includedAccountsPerUser]] connected account per user and Business [[commercial.entitlement.business.includedAccountsPerUser]], so a consultant who wants email, LinkedIn and WhatsApp side by side needs Business. Starter has no inbox.</Step>
</Steps>

**An example routine (Customermates Cloud, uses hosted AI credits).** A routine is a saved instruction for Mate, the assistant, with a trigger. Set it to repeat every week on Monday at 08:00 with this instruction:

> List my deals in stage "Proposal sent" whose last update is older than ten days. For each one, write a short follow-up email in the existing thread with the main contact and save it as a draft. Do not send anything.

The last sentence matters. A routine runs as its owner and may send messages without asking, so an instruction that should only draft has to say so. Limits and run statuses are on the [routines documentation](/docs/app-routines). If you prefer your own AI client, Claude, ChatGPT and other MCP clients can read and update the same records through the Customermates MCP server, using your AI provider instead of hosted credits.

Four consultants on Pro cost [[commercial.price.pro.monthly.seats.4]] per month ([[commercial.price.pro.monthly]] per user), which includes the inbox and monthly hosted AI credits per active user. Without the inbox, Starter costs [[commercial.price.starter.monthly.seats.4]] per month. The full feature list is on [all features](/features/all).

## Where Customermates does not fit

Some CRM examples above need things Customermates does not do. Be clear about them before you pick it:

- **No email campaigns or automated sequences.** Messages go out one at a time, drafted or sent from the inbox. For newsletters or multi-step outreach, use an email-marketing tool next to the CRM.
- **No support for phone-heavy sales.** There is no telephony or call logging integration, and no mobile app. The property agent above needs another tool.
- **No ticket queues.** There is no help-desk module with SLAs, so the support desk example needs a help-desk tool.
- **No built-in task due dates.** Tasks have relations, assignees and notes; add a custom date field if you need one, and filter on it.
- **No quotes, invoices or client portal.** Keep those in your accounting or proposal tool.

If most of your work sits in those areas, look for a CRM suite whose edition includes a help desk, calling or quoting. [How to choose a CRM](/blog/how-to-choose-crm) gives you a scoring sheet for that decision.

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What is a CRM and what are examples?">
A CRM (customer relationship management) system stores your contacts, companies, deals and conversations in one place, so everyone sees the same history and the next step. Examples of CRM software are Salesforce Sales Cloud, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Sales, Zoho CRM, Pipedrive, SuiteCRM and Customermates. Examples of CRM in use are a consultant's Monday proposal review, an agency's shared client history and a sales team's weekly forecast.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the top 5 CRM tools?">
There is no neutral public count of users across all vendors. Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics 365, Zoho CRM and Pipedrive are the five names that appear in nearly every comparison. Popularity says little about fit: a three-person consultancy and a 500-seat sales floor need different tools.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is Excel a CRM software?">
No. Excel is a spreadsheet, so it can hold a contact list and a simple deal sheet, but it does not log emails, remind anyone of a next step or keep one history per customer when several people edit it. Many small teams start there anyway; [the Excel CRM template guide](/blog/excel-crm-template) shows how far a workbook goes.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does a CRM system cost?">
In October 2026, free plans covered 2 users at HubSpot and 3 at Zoho CRM. Paid list prices ran from about €14 to $65 per user per month at entry level and up to $550 for Salesforce's top edition, with most mid and top tiers billed annually. Setup and training come on top.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the four types of CRM?">
Operational CRM runs daily sales and service work, analytical CRM turns records into reports, collaborative CRM shares one customer history across teams, and strategic CRM aligns the business around long-term customer value. Most products combine the first three.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Where is CRM used?">
Anywhere a business has repeat contact with the same people: B2B sales, agencies and consultancies, recruiting, real estate, financial advice, support teams and membership organizations. The application of CRM changes by business, the principle does not.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Will CRM be replaced by AI?">
Not replaced, but changed. AI assistants and agents still need a place that holds customers, deals and conversations, and that place is the CRM. What changes is who does the data entry: in an [AI CRM](/blog/ai-crm), an assistant drafts follow-ups and updates records, and external AI clients work in the CRM through MCP.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is SAP a CRM?">
SAP is a software vendor, not a type of software. CRM manages customer relationships and sales, while ERP runs finance, purchasing and operations, and large vendors sell products in both categories. The differences are explained in [CRM and ERP](/blog/crm-and-erp).
</FaqItem>
</Faq>
