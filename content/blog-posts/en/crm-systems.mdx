# CRM Systems: What They Do, the Four Types and Common Examples

A CRM system is software that keeps contacts, companies, deals and the conversations around them as linked records, so anyone on the team can open a client and see where things stand and what happens next. A spreadsheet can hold the same names. What it cannot do well is link a person to a company and three open deals, keep the email history next to them, and show the whole pipeline by stage without someone rebuilding a pivot table.

Almost every CRM system you can buy today is an operational CRM at heart, with some reporting on top. The "four types" you read about describe jobs a CRM does, not four separate products. For most teams under twenty people, the useful question is which daily work the system has to carry and what that costs at the plan that actually includes it.

Customermates, an open-source CRM, publishes this guide. Other vendors are listed alphabetically and not ranked; Customermates has its own labelled section. Vendor facts were checked on 8 October 2026 on each vendor's own pages, linked at the end.

## What a CRM system stores

Every CRM system is built from a handful of record types that point at each other. The names differ by vendor; the model is the same.

| Record | What it holds | Example at a 12-person consultancy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | A person: name, email, phone, role, notes | Anna Becker, Head of Operations |
| Organization (company, account) | The business the contacts work for | Becker Logistik GmbH, 3 contacts linked |
| Deal (opportunity) | A piece of potential revenue with a stage and a value | "Process audit Q1", stage Proposal, €18,000 |
| Activity or conversation | Emails, calls, meetings, messages, linked to the records above | Last email from Anna, 6 days ago |
| Task | A piece of work someone has to do, linked to a record | "Send revised scope", assigned to Jonas |

The links are what make it a system. Open Becker Logistik and you see its three contacts, the open proposal and the last conversation. Open the pipeline and you see that deal next to every other one in Proposal.

### Is Excel a CRM?

No, but it is where most CRMs start. A spreadsheet works while one person owns the list and nobody needs the history behind a row. It stops working when two people update the same client, when you want to know who last spoke to Anna and what was said, or when a deal moves stages and nobody updates the sheet. If you are not there yet, a structured sheet is a fine first step: our [Excel CRM template](/blog/excel-crm-template) shows the columns that make the later move into a CRM painless.

## The four types of CRM system

The classification comes from CRM textbooks and analyst reports, and vendors use it loosely. Treat it as a map of jobs.

| Type | Job | Typical modules | Who needs it first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Operational CRM | Run the daily customer-facing work | Contact management, pipeline, email and calendar sync, task lists, marketing campaigns, service tickets | Everyone who sells or serves clients |
| Analytical CRM | Turn the collected data into answers | Dashboards, win and loss analysis, segmentation, revenue reporting | Founders and sales leads once there is enough history to analyse |
| Collaborative CRM | Share one customer view across people and channels | Shared conversation history, notes, handoffs between sales, delivery and support | Teams where more than one person talks to the same client |
| Strategic CRM | Plan the business around customer relationships | Customer lifetime value, retention goals, account plans | Larger firms with dedicated account management |

Some lists replace strategic CRM with social CRM, meaning social media channels inside the customer record. Either way, a product sold as "a CRM" today usually bundles operational and collaborative functions, adds some analytics, and leaves strategy to people.

### Operational CRM in detail

Operational CRM is the part you touch every day. It is usually split into three areas:

- **Sales force automation:** contacts, organizations, deals and the pipeline, with activities logged against them and the next step visible on every deal.
- **Marketing automation:** capturing leads from forms and campaigns, sending newsletters and nurture emails, handing qualified leads to sales.
- **Service automation:** support tickets, a shared help inbox, case history and handoff to the account owner.

A concrete operational day at a five-person agency: an inquiry arrives by email and becomes a contact and a deal in the first stage. The account lead moves it to Proposal after the call, the pipeline view shows the team which proposals are open, and when the client replies on LinkedIn the message lands next to the same deal. Nothing about that requires analytics or strategy. It requires the records, the stages and the conversations in one place.

Few small teams need all three operational areas from one vendor. A consultancy usually needs sales force automation and conversation history, keeps newsletters in a dedicated email tool, and has no ticket queue at all. A software company with paying users needs service automation early. Buy for the area that carries your revenue.

### Which type does a small team need?

| If your situation is… | Start with | Add later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deals are lost because follow-ups slip | Operational: pipeline with stages and conversation history | Reporting on stage conversion once you have a few months of data |
| Several people talk to the same client | Collaborative: shared history and assigned users on each deal | Clear rules on who owns the next step |
| Most revenue comes from inbound marketing | Operational with marketing automation | Analytical attribution reports |
| You sell to existing customers more than new ones | Collaborative and service functions | Strategic account plans |

Second, where the features sit: if the plan you can afford lacks email sync or automation, the real entry price is the next tier. ## How to choose a CRM system

<Steps>
  <Step title="Write down the daily job">List the five to seven things the system must do every week, for example "see every open proposal", "keep LinkedIn messages with the client" or "hand a client from sales to delivery". Everything else is optional.</Step>
  <Step title="Set the hard limits">Budget per month for your real team size, where the data may be stored, whether you need to self-host, and any integration you cannot work without. Drop every system that fails one.</Step>
  <Step title="Price the right tier">Find the cheapest plan that contains all your must-haves, at the billing cadence you will use, and add onboarding fees.</Step>
  <Step title="Trial two systems with your own data">Import a slice of real contacts and open deals, and run a normal week in each. The people who will use it daily decide.</Step>
  <Step title="Move one record type at a time">Clean duplicates first, import organizations, then contacts, then deals, and check the links after each step.</Step>
</Steps>

For a weighted scorecard, read [how to choose a CRM](/blog/how-to-choose-crm).

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
<FaqItem question="What are examples of CRM systems?">
Widely used CRM systems include Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Sales, SAP Sales Cloud, HubSpot, Zoho CRM and Pipedrive. CentralStationCRM is a simple CRM made in Germany for small teams, and SuiteCRM is an open-source system you can host yourself. Customermates, which publishes this guide, is an open-source CRM for small client-facing teams.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What are the top 5 CRM systems?">
There is no neutral top five, because the right system depends on team size and the job. The names that appear on many published lists are Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics 365, Zoho CRM and Pipedrive. A five-person consultancy and a 500-person sales organization would not pick from that list in the same order, so start from your must-haves rather than a ranking.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is an operational CRM?">
An operational CRM runs the daily customer-facing work: sales force automation (contacts, deals, pipeline), marketing automation (lead capture, campaigns) and service automation (tickets, case history). Analytical CRM, by contrast, evaluates the data that operational CRM collects. Most products sold as CRM software are operational at their core.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What is a collaborative CRM?">
A collaborative CRM gives everyone who deals with a client the same history: sales, delivery and support see the same conversations, notes and open deals, so a handoff does not lose context. In practice this is a set of functions inside an operational CRM, such as shared conversation history and assigned users per deal.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is SAP a CRM system?">
SAP is mainly known for ERP, but it sells CRM systems too. SAP Sales Cloud is SAP's sales CRM and part of the SAP Customer Experience portfolio, alongside SAP Service Cloud. SAP describes it as integrated with SAP ERP and SAP S/4HANA, so it makes most sense for companies that already run SAP.
</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How much does a CRM system cost per month?">
For five users, list prices checked on 8 October 2026 ranged from free plans with user caps (HubSpot's free tools for 2 users, Zoho CRM's free edition for 3) to $325 a month for Dynamics 365 Sales Professional paid yearly. Customermates Pro for five users is [[commercial.price.pro.monthly.seats.5]] a month. Check whether the price you see requires annual billing.
</FaqItem>
